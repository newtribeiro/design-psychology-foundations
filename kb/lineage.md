# Lineage — art & design movements behind the principles
Documented history and interpretation, not graded findings. Use for: where a visual convention comes from, what a style signals, what got lost on the way to digital. Deep study lives in the companion designer-growth skill (Module H).

## Renaissance perspective & composition · c.1415–1500s · Florence / Italy
- Figures: Filippo Brunelleschi, Leon Battista Alberti, Masaccio, Donatello, Leonardo da Vinci · Works: Brunelleschi's Baptistery perspective panel (c.1415, lost); Alberti, De pictura (1435); Masaccio, Holy Trinity (1425–27)
- Intent: A geometric method for placing the viewer in a single, measurable space so painted narratives read as continuous with the real world; Alberti codified it as a teachable procedure.
- Signals today: Interpretive: depth, realism and 'serious' authority; a centred vanishing point still reads as order and control.
- Into digital: Drop shadows, z-axis elevation, parallax and 3D product renders reuse perspective and chiaroscuro cues to imply hierarchy and layering (e.g. Material elevation, card stacks).
- Lost: Perspective was a narrative device for one fixed viewpoint; UI depth is often decorative and ignores where attention actually goes.
- Principles: ↳ Visual Hierarchy (Vanishing point and scale order what is seen first); ↳ Centre-Stage Effect (Central placement signals importance in perspective compositions); ↳ Contrast (Chiaroscuro uses light–dark contrast to model form); ⟂ Rule of Thirds (Renaissance composition was centric, not thirds-based); ⟂ Golden Ratio (Golden-ratio claims about Renaissance art are largely retrofitted)
- Watch: Treating Western single-point perspective as the 'natural' way of seeing erases other pictorial systems (e.g. East Asian oblique projection).
- Sources: https://smarthistory.org/linear-perspective ; https://www.britannica.com/art/linear-perspective ; https://smarthistory.org/early-applications-of-linear-perspective

## Arts & Crafts · c.1860–1910s · Britain, then Europe & USA
- Figures: John Ruskin, William Morris, Philip Webb, Edward Burne-Jones, C.R. Ashbee · Works: Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. founded (1861); Kelmscott Press founded (1891); Kelmscott Chaucer (1896)
- Intent: A social protest against degraded industrial goods and alienated labour: reunite designer and maker and restore the dignity of handwork, inspired by Ruskin's moral critique.
- Signals today: Interpretive: craft, authenticity, honesty of materials, slowness — today often read as premium or artisanal.
- Into digital: Echoed in craft-and-care brand voices, hand-drawn illustration systems, editorial typography revivals and 'made by humans' positioning; Kelmscott's page-as-a-whole thinking prefigures layout systems.
- Lost: The socialist goal of fair labour was dropped; the look now sells premium goods, the very contradiction Morris faced.
- Principles: ↳ Noble Edge Effect (Ethical making signals a company's values); ↳ Labor Illusion (Visible effort raises perceived value of crafted things); ↳ Sensory Appeal (Texture, pattern and material richness invite touch); ↳ Visual Style Connotation (Hand-made look connotes authenticity and care); ⟂ Halo Effect (Craft aesthetics can mask poor function or labour practices)
- Watch: 'Artisanal' styling on mass-produced or exploitative products is craft-washing, the opposite of the movement's intent.
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/art/Arts-and-Crafts-movement ; https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/introducing-william-morris ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelmscott_Press

## Art Nouveau / Jugendstil / Secession · c.1890–1914 · Belgium, France, Austria, Germany, Scotland, USA
- Figures: Victor Horta, Alphonse Mucha, Hector Guimard, Henry van de Velde, Gustav Klimt · Works: Horta, Hôtel Tassel, Brussels (1893); Mucha, Gismonda poster (1894–95); Bing's gallery L'Art Nouveau, Paris (1895)
- Intent: Create a modern style free of historical revival, derived from nature, and erase the hierarchy between fine and applied arts (Gesamtkunstwerk).
- Signals today: Interpretive: organic elegance, romance, luxury, nostalgia for the belle époque; also 'hand-made' warmth.
- Into digital: Seen in botanical illustration systems, ornamental display type, Mucha-style framed portraits in games and cosmetics branding; the 'total design' idea maps onto end-to-end brand systems.
- Lost: The goal of a unified, total environment shrank to surface ornament and nostalgic poster pastiche.
- Principles: ↳ Curvature Preference (Whiplash curves exploit the general preference for curves); ↳ Fractal Fluency (Botanical branching echoes nature's self-similar patterns); ↳ Sensory Appeal (Ornament and material richness aim at pleasure); ⟂ Cognitive Load (Dense ornament competes with content legibility); ↳ Visual Style Connotation (Organic line now connotes luxury and nostalgia)
- Watch: Mucha pastiche is a fantasy-and-cosmetics cliché; ornamental lettering easily fails legibility and accessibility.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/art-nouveau ; https://www.vam.ac.uk/collections/art-nouveau ; https://muchafoundation.org/gallery/browse-works/object/21

## De Stijl (Neoplasticism) · 1917–1932 · Netherlands
- Figures: Theo van Doesburg, Piet Mondrian, Gerrit Rietveld, Vilmos Huszár, J.J.P. Oud · Works: De Stijl journal (1917–32); Rietveld, Red Blue Chair (1918–23); Rietveld, Schröder House, Utrecht (1924)
- Intent: A universal visual language of straight lines, planes and primaries that would express harmony and equilibrium applicable to art and to life after the chaos of the First World War.
- Signals today: Interpretive: rationality, modernity, playful order; now often read as 'art-school modern' or retro-graphic.
- Into digital: Rectilinear modular layouts, bento grids, tiling dashboards and primary-colour accent systems; the reduction of form to a small set of elements anticipates component systems.
- Lost: A utopian, almost spiritual quest for universal harmony became a decorative colour-block motif.
- Principles: ↳ Law of Prägnanz (Reduction to simplest planes and lines); ↳ Visual Balance (Asymmetric equilibrium of unequal colour areas); ↳ Law of Similarity (Few repeated elements build a coherent system); ↳ Occam's Razor (Strip form to the fewest necessary elements); ⟂ Fitts's Law (Rietveld's chair shows form-first ergonomics trade-offs)
- Watch: Mondrian blocks are one of the most clichéd 'modern art' references; using them as decoration flattens a serious theory.
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/topic/De-Stijl-art ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4044 ; https://smarthistory.org/de-stijl-part-i-total-purity/

## Russian Constructivism · c.1915–1930s · Russia / Soviet Union
- Figures: Vladimir Tatlin, Aleksandr Rodchenko, Varvara Stepanova, Liubov Popova, El Lissitzky · Works: Lissitzky, Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge (1919–20); Lissitzky, Of Two Squares (1922); Lissitzky & Mayakovsky, For the Voice (1923)
- Intent: Replace art-for-art's-sake with the 'artist-engineer' producing useful objects, propaganda, typography and textiles for a new socialist society.
- Signals today: Interpretive: revolution, urgency, disruption, collective power; red/black diagonals now read as activist or edgy.
- Into digital: Diagonal compositions, photomontage, bold sans headlines and red/black palettes in campaign sites, music/streaming promos and 'disruptor' brand launches.
- Lost: The anti-commercial political programme became a style used to sell consumer goods and, ironically, luxury streetwear.
- Principles: ↳ Visual Hierarchy (Scale and diagonals drive the eye through messages); ↳ Von Restorff Effect (Red wedge isolates the key element against grey/black); ↳ Framing (Graphic form framed political choices as us-versus-them); ↳ Contrast (Extreme scale and colour contrast for instant impact); ↳ Visual Style Connotation (Diagonal red-black now connotes revolution or edge)
- Watch: Borrowing Soviet propaganda graphics for products can trivialise state violence and reads as tone-deaf to some audiences.
- Sources: https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/constructivism ; https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/c/constructivism ; https://www.britannica.com/biography/El-Lissitzky

## Bauhaus · 1919–1933 · Germany (Weimar, Dessau, Berlin); diaspora to USA
- Figures: Walter Gropius, Johannes Itten, Josef Albers, László Moholy-Nagy, Herbert Bayer · Works: Bauhaus founded in Weimar (1919); Dessau building by Gropius (1925–26); Kandinsky, Point and Line to Plane (Bauhausbücher 9, 1926)
- Intent: Unite art, craft and industry in one school so artists could design good, reproducible everyday objects and buildings for modern society; the preliminary course trained perception of form, colour and material.
- Signals today: Interpretive: functional modernity, rational design, 'design-school' credibility; also austerity and coldness to some.
- Into digital: Foundation of design education and of UI basics — grids, geometric sans type, colour theory exercises, form-follows-function rhetoric and component thinking in design systems.
- Lost: Bauhaus was experimental, plural and often handmade; it is now flattened into a geometric-sans-plus-primaries look.
- Principles: ↳ Aesthetic-Usability Effect (Fusing beauty and utility in everyday objects); ↳ Law of Prägnanz (Elementary forms: circle, square, triangle); ↳ Colour–Emotion Associations (Itten and Kandinsky theorised colour-form-emotion links); ↳ Occam's Razor (Reduction to essential form for industrial production); ⟂ Survivorship Bias (Canon remembers male masters, forgets weaving workshop women)
- Watch: 'Bauhaus style' as a font-and-primaries template erases its women and its experimental craft side; Kandinsky's colour-shape correspondences are not empirical facts.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-bauhaus-1919-1933 ; https://www.bauhaus.de/en/discover/article/the-bauhaus-idea-and-programme/ ; https://www.lars-mueller-publishers.com/point-and-line-plane

## Isotype (Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics) · 1925–1971 · Vienna → The Hague → Oxford
- Figures: Otto Neurath, Marie Neurath (Reidemeister), Gerd Arntz · Works: Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum, Vienna (1925); Gesellschaft und Wirtschaft atlas (1930); Renamed Isotype (1935)
- Intent: Democratic visual education: make social and economic statistics understandable to working people without literacy or numeracy barriers, using repeatable pictograms where more icons mean more quantity.
- Signals today: Interpretive: clarity, civic trust, neutrality, friendly rationality.
- Into digital: Direct ancestor of pictogram sets, icon systems, infographics, unit charts and wayfinding icons; the 'transformer' role prefigures the information/UX designer.
- Lost: The educational, democratic purpose is often lost; icon grids now decorate rather than count.
- Principles: ↳ Picture Superiority Effect (Pictures recalled better than numbers or words); ↳ Recognition Over Recall (Recognisable pictograms replace reading tables); ↳ Law of Similarity (Identical repeated units encode equal quantities); ↳ Chunking (Counting in units chunks large numbers); ↳ Curse of Knowledge ('Transformer' role translates expert data for laypeople)
- Watch: Pictograms encode cultural defaults (gender, body, class); 'universal' icons often are not.
- Sources: https://isotype.univie.ac.at/en/abstract ; https://gerdarntz.org/content/gerd-arntz.html

## Swiss / International Typographic Style · c.1950–1970s (Ulm HfG 1953–68) · Switzerland (Basel, Zurich), Germany (Ulm)
- Figures: Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, Emil Ruder, Max Bill, Otl Aicher · Works: Müller-Brockmann, Musica Viva posters (from 1950); Helvetica (1957); Neue Grafik journal (1958–65)
- Intent: Objective, rational communication: grids, sans-serif type and documentary photography to convey information clearly and neutrally, as a post-war ethic of clarity over persuasion.
- Signals today: Interpretive: neutrality, precision, institutional trust, 'international' professionalism.
- Into digital: The default grammar of UI: 8/12-column grids, flush-left ragged-right type, sans-serif system fonts, generous whitespace; Ulm's systems thinking feeds design systems and the Braun → product-design lineage.
- Lost: 'Neutral' was itself an ideology; the style's systems discipline is copied more than its rigour about content.
- Principles: ↳ Visual Hierarchy (Type scale and grid structure order information); ↳ Law of Proximity (Grid spacing groups related content); ↳ Processing Fluency (Consistent grids and clean type ease reading); ↳ Cognitive Load (Removing ornament reduces extraneous processing); ↳ Prototypicality (Became the prototype of 'professional' layout); ⟂ Familiarity Bias (Ubiquity makes it the uncritical default)
- Watch: Swiss minimalism is the corporate default; it can feel cold and culturally specific, and 'neutral' is never neutral.
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Typographic-Style ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_M%C3%BCller-Brockmann ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulm_School_of_Design ; https://letterformarchive.org/news/legacies-of-swiss-style-tyografische-monatsblatter/

## Mid-century corporate modernism · c.1950–1980 · USA, Italy
- Figures: Paul Rand, Ivan Chermayeff, Tom Geismar, Massimo Vignelli, Bob Noorda · Works: Rand, IBM logo (1956; striped 1972); Chermayeff & Geismar, Chase Manhattan symbol (1960); Rand, UPS (1961)
- Intent: Bring modernist reduction into business: simple, systematic identities and standards manuals to make large organisations and public systems coherent and legible.
- Signals today: Interpretive: stability, competence, scale, institutional trust; also 'establishment'.
- Into digital: Brand guidelines became design systems; logo systems, app icons and transit-style wayfinding in apps descend from standards manuals; Rand's NeXT logo (1986) links directly to tech branding.
- Lost: Manuals were about governance and public legibility; today the reduction is often chased as a trend (blanding).
- Principles: ↳ Authority Bias (Systematic identity signals a competent institution); ↳ Familiarity Bias (Consistency over decades builds recognition); ↳ Law of Prägnanz (Abstract simple marks survive reduction); ⟂ Mental Model (Vignelli map traded geography for clarity; riders objected); ↳ Halo Effect (Good identity lends credibility to the whole company)
- Watch: Copying the flat-mark-plus-sans formula produces interchangeable 'blands'; the Vignelli map shows that elegance can clash with users' mental models.
- Sources: https://www.moma.org/collection/works/89300 ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/89303 ; https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/symbols-and-survival ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Rand

## Japanese Ma, wabi-sabi & Mingei · Ma/wabi-sabi: centuries-old; Mingei coined 1925, museum 1936 · Japan (with Korea in Yanagi's collecting)
- Figures: Yanagi Sōetsu, Hamada Shōji, Kawai Kanjirō, Bernard Leach, Arata Isozaki · Works: Term 'mingei' coined (1925); Japan Folk Crafts Museum (Nihon Mingeikan), Tokyo (1936); Yanagi, The Unknown Craftsman (Eng. 1972)
- Intent: Mingei valued anonymous, everyday, functional craft as 'beauty of use' against industrial standardisation; Ma names the meaningful interval of space and time between things.
- Signals today: Interpretive: calm, restraint, humility, imperfection-as-honesty, premium minimalism.
- Into digital: Whitespace-as-content, pause and pacing in motion design, 'calm technology' and quiet product aesthetics; mingei's 'beauty of use' parallels utility-first product craft.
- Lost: Ma is temporal and relational, not just empty margin; wabi-sabi is reduced to beige minimalism and 'distressed' textures.
- Principles: ↳ Visual Hierarchy (Emptiness frames and elevates the essential); ↳ Cognitive Load (Restraint reduces visual and decision clutter); ↳ Chronoception (Ma includes intervals of time, i.e. pacing); ↳ Aesthetic-Usability Effect ('Beauty of use' fuses function and pleasure); ⟂ Visual Style Connotation (Western 'zen' cliché misreads the concepts)
- Watch: 'Zen' styling is an orientalist cliché; Yanagi's own Korea writings are criticised as colonial aesthetics, so cite mingei with that context.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingei ; https://mingeikan.or.jp/?lang=en ; https://www.archpaper.com/?p=263191

## Islamic geometric pattern & calligraphy · c.8th century onward (girih from c.13th c.) · Middle East, North Africa, Iberia, Central & South Asia
- Figures: Anonymous master builders and craftsmen, Calligraphers (e.g. Ibn Muqla, proportioned scripts) · Works: Alhambra, Granada (mainly 13th–14th c.); Darb-i Imam shrine girih, Isfahan (1453); Met — Damascus Room (1707)
- Intent: Sacred and civic ornament that avoids figurative imagery in religious contexts and expresses order and infinity through geometry built from circle and compass; calligraphy elevates the written word.
- Signals today: Interpretive: order, infinity, spirituality, sophistication of craft and mathematics.
- Into digital: Tessellations in pattern libraries, generative/procedural backgrounds, rosette-based icon geometry, and Arabic-script typography in multi-script design systems.
- Lost: Pattern is lifted from meaning and context; calligraphy carrying sacred text is used as mere texture.
- Principles: ↳ Symmetry Preference (Rotational and mirror symmetry at every scale); ↳ Unity-in-Variety (Few rules yield endlessly varied patterns); ↳ Law of Similarity (Repeated units read as one surface); ↳ Fractal Fluency (Nested scales of pattern resemble self-similarity); ⟂ Golden Ratio (Proportion was compass-based, not golden-ratio derived)
- Watch: Using Qur'anic or Arabic calligraphy as decoration, or 'arabesque' as exotic shorthand, is a common appropriation and sacrilege risk.
- Sources: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/geometric-patterns-in-islamic-art ; https://www.nature.com/news/2007/070219/full/news070219-9.html ; https://www.metmuseum.org/learn/educators/lesson-plans/geometric-design-in-islamic-art

## Brazilian Concrete & Neo-Concrete art · 1952–1960s (Ruptura 1952; Neo-Concrete Manifesto 1959) · Brazil (São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, Recife)
- Figures: Waldemar Cordeiro, Geraldo de Barros, Judith Lauand, Lygia Clark, Hélio Oiticica · Works: Ruptura exhibition & manifesto, MAM São Paulo (1952); Neo-Concrete Manifesto, Jornal do Brasil (1959); Lygia Pape, Book of Creation (1959)
- Intent: Concretists pursued objective geometric abstraction for a modernising Brazil; Neo-Concretists rejected pure rationalism for participation, the body and lived experience; Magalhães applied modernism to national identity design.
- Signals today: Interpretive: optimistic modernity, tropical rationalism, participation and play; national modernist pride.
- Into digital: Participatory, manipulable objects anticipate interaction design; Magalhães's moiré banknotes and identities show systematic national branding; Burle Marx's wave pavement is a canonical pattern reference.
- Lost: The Neo-Concrete emphasis on the participant's body and agency is lost when it is used only as geometric style.
- Principles: ↳ IKEA Effect (Participants complete works by manipulating them); ↳ Feedback Loop (Bichos respond to the user's handling); ↳ Law of Prägnanz (Concrete art rooted in simple geometric form); ↳ Curvature Preference (Burle Marx's organic curves versus orthogonal modernism); ⟂ Survivorship Bias (Global canon under-tells Latin American modernism)
- Watch: Copacabana waves and 'tropical modernism' are tourist clichés; credit specific artists rather than a vague 'Brazilian vibe'.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grupo_Ruptura ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Concrete_Movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alo%C3%ADsio_Magalh%C3%A3es ; https://www.creativereview.co.uk/robertos-rio-celebrating-the-artist-behind-copacabanas-patterned-pavements

## Postmodernism, Memphis & New Wave typography · c.1968–1990s (Memphis 1980–c.1988) · Italy, Switzerland, USA
- Figures: Ettore Sottsass, Michele De Lucchi, Wolfgang Weingart, April Greiman, Rudy VanderLans · Works: Sottsass, Valentine typewriter for Olivetti (1969); Memphis first show, Milan (1981); Sottsass, Carlton room divider (1981)
- Intent: Rebellion against modernist rationalism and 'good taste': irony, colour, kitsch and layered, intuitive typography; early adoption of the Macintosh as an expressive tool.
- Signals today: Interpretive: playfulness, irreverence, 80s/90s nostalgia, creative freedom.
- Into digital: Bitmap fonts and Mac-native type (Emigre), layered expressive web/brand work, 2010s 'Memphis revival' illustrations, brutalist and anti-design websites.
- Lost: A critique of modernist dogma became a candy-coloured trend with no argument behind it.
- Principles: ↳ Von Restorff Effect (Clashing patterns make objects unforgettable); ↳ Complexity–Arousal Curve (High complexity raises arousal and interest); ↳ Delighters (Humour and surprise as design values); ⟂ Cognitive Load (Layered type costs legibility); ⟂ Bandwagon Effect (Memphis revival spread as trend without intent)
- Watch: Memphis squiggles and confetti are a startup-illustration cliché; layered type can break readability and accessibility.
- Sources: https://designmuseum.org/designers/ettore-sottsass ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/292736 ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/101653 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Weingart

## Digital: skeuomorphism → flat → material → glass · 1981–present · USA (Xerox PARC, Apple, Google), global
- Figures: Xerox PARC Star team, Susan Kare, Jony Ive, Matías Duarte (Material) · Works: Xerox Star 8010 (1981); Kare, Macintosh icon sketchbook (1982) / Macintosh (1984); iOS 7 (June 2013)
- Intent: Desktop metaphors and realistic icons taught novices how computers worked; flat design (iOS 7) removed ornament once users were literate and screens were high-density; Material re-added depth as a rule-based system.
- Signals today: Interpretive: skeuomorphism = friendly/tangible, flat = modern/efficient, glass and depth = premium/futuristic.
- Into digital: This is the native lineage of UI itself: metaphors, icons, elevation tokens, shadows, translucency layers.
- Lost: Flat design often removed signifiers along with ornament, hurting discoverability; neumorphism repeated the mistake with low contrast.
- Principles: ↳ Skeuomorphism (Real-world metaphors taught first computer users); ⟂ Signifiers (Flat design stripped affordance cues from controls); ↳ Mental Model (Desktop, folder and trash map familiar office models); ⟂ Discoverability (Flat buttons and glass blur what's clickable); ↳ MAYA Principle (Each shift stays novel yet acceptable); ⟂ Contrast (Neumorphism and glass struggle with contrast)
- Watch: Chasing platform aesthetics (neumorphism, glass) without contrast and signifier checks creates accessibility failures.
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/technology/Xerox-Star ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/188382 ; https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2013/06/10Apple-Unveils-iOS-7/ ; https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/web-design-history/material-design-2014

## Pop Art · mid-1950s–1970s · UK, USA
- Figures: Richard Hamilton, Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Claes Oldenburg · Works: Warhol, Campbell's Soup Cans (1962); Lichtenstein, Whaam! (1963)
- Intent: Take mass-produced imagery — ads, comics, packaging, celebrity — as legitimate subject and method, commenting (often ambiguously) on consumer culture and reproduction.
- Signals today: Interpretive: fun, brashness, consumer culture, irony, nostalgia.
- Into digital: Halftone/Ben-Day textures, comic panels, sticker and meme aesthetics, serial grid repetition in social and campaign design.
- Lost: The ambiguous critique of consumption became straightforward consumer branding.
- Principles: ↳ Familiarity Bias (Recycles already-familiar commercial imagery); ↳ Law of Similarity (Serial repetition of identical units); ↳ Von Restorff Effect (Flat bold colour and enlarged dots stand out); ↳ Peak Shift Effect (Exaggerated comic features amplify recognisability)
- Watch: Comic and halftone pastiche is a cliché; Lichtenstein's sources raise artist-credit questions worth remembering.
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/art/Pop-art ; https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79809
