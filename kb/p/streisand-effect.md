## Streisand Effect
_Censoring spreads the info_ · cycle: 🔮 Meaning · cluster: C7 · evidence: Practitioner
- Definition: Trying to suppress, hide or censor information draws far more attention to it than leaving it alone would have.
- Mechanism (why it happens): Suppression signals that the information is valuable, which triggers curiosity and reactance (a threat to people's freedom to know). It also creates a new, shareable injustice story. Networked sharing then amplifies the outrage.
- Origin & key evidence:
  - Streisand (2003) tried to restrict an aerial photo of her Malibu home that was part of a coastal-erosion archive; the attempt brought it far more views. Jansen & Martin (2015, *International Journal of Communication* 9) frame this as "censorship backfire" and list five tactics censors use to reduce outrage. https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/view/2498
  - Hobbs & Roberts (2018, *American Political Science Review*): a natural experiment on the sudden 2014 Instagram block in China. Many users adopted VPNs, new Twitter sign-ups jumped by over 600% on the block day, and some of these initially apolitical users later read blocked political Wikipedia pages and followed activists. https://networkscienceinstitute.org/publications/how-sudden-censorship-can-increase-access-to-information ; working paper with figures: https://pages.ucsd.edu/~whobbs/selfiecensorship.pdf
  - Nabi (2014, *First Monday* 19(11)), "Censorship is futile": case studies of blocks in Pakistan and Turkey. Search interest in censored topics and in circumvention tools rose after blocks, and one censored Turkish video gained daily views. Sparse data, preliminary. https://www.firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/5525/4155?inline=1
  - The AACS "09 F9" key (2007): DMCA takedowns on Digg led users to post the key everywhere, and Digg reversed course (anecdotal case). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AACS_encryption_key_controversy
- Evidence grade: Practitioner. Support is mostly case studies plus two observational or quasi-experimental studies (Hobbs & Roberts; Nabi). Neither is a controlled experiment, and the Chinese case shows a displaced-access effect, not attention to a specific suppressed item. Survivorship bias is likely, because suppression that worked doesn't make the news.
- Design applications:
  - Moderation transparency: explain removals and offer appeals instead of silent deletion. Signal: appeal rate and the volume of public complaints.
  - Changelogs: disclose a removed feature or a price change up front. Signal: sentiment spikes on social media and support tickets after the change.
  - Incident status pages: communicate outages openly. Signal: duplicate support tickets during incidents.
  - Avoid aggressive takedowns of user reviews. Signal: review-site rating trend.
- Real product examples: The Digg/AACS 2007 revolt (above). Companies whose attempts to suppress negative reviews or press drew wider coverage (no specific URL verified).
- Enterprise/B2B note: Hiding known defects or limits from B2B customers (e.g. a firmware issue) tends to come out through customer networks. Proactive advisories build trust.
- Ethics/watch-out: Transparency is the ethical default. Don't respond by suppressing more quietly.
- Contexts: content/copy, stakeholder-communication, branding, offboarding, notifications
