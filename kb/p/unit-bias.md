## Unit Bias
_One unit feels like the right amount_ · cycle: 🔮 Meaning · cluster: C4 · evidence: Moderate
- Definition: People treat one presented unit (portion, package, item) as the appropriate amount to consume or complete, regardless of its size.
- Mechanism (why it happens): A culturally learned heuristic that "one unit is the right amount" provides a cheap stopping rule (System 1), so the size of the unit, rather than need, governs consumption or completion.
- Origin & key evidence:
  - Geier, Rozin & Doros (2006), Psychological Science: people took more snacks (M&Ms, pretzels, Tootsie Rolls) when the unit or serving spoon was larger — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16771803/
  - Broad portion-size literature shows larger portions increase intake (FoodNavigator summary) — https://foodnavigator.com/Article/2006/08/02/Large-portion-sizes-result-in-more-consumption-confirms-study
- Evidence grade: Moderate — portion-size effect is robust; the "unit" framing specifically rests on fewer studies and is mostly food-domain.
- Design applications:
  - Define one unit of work as the right size (one lesson, one checklist item) — completion rate per unit.
  - Daily goals sized as a single "unit" (one streak) — retention and goal completion.
  - Pricing unit framing (per seat, per usage-hour) — conversion by unit framing variant.
  - Chunk long forms into "one step" units — step completion and drop-off.
- Real product examples: Duolingo lesson as a single unit; Headspace "one session" (no source URL verified).
- Enterprise/B2B note: Defining the billing or work unit (per device, per job) shapes how much customers buy or consider "enough" usage.
- Ethics/watch-out: Oversized default units (autoplay episodes, large bundles) drive overconsumption.
- Contexts: onboarding, pricing, gamification, retention, forms
