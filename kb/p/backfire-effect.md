## Backfire Effect
_Challenges harden beliefs_ · cycle: ⏰ Time · cluster: C9 · evidence: Contested
- Definition: The claim that correcting someone's mistaken belief, especially an identity-linked one, can make them hold it more strongly instead of updating.
- Mechanism (why it happens): The proposed mechanism is motivated reasoning: a threat to identity triggers counter-arguing, which rehearses and strengthens the original belief. A related "familiarity backfire" holds that repeating a myth while correcting it makes the myth more fluent. Current evidence suggests both are rare; the more reliable problem is the "continued influence effect" (corrected myths still partly shape reasoning).
- Origin & key evidence:
  - Nyhan & Reifler (2010), Political Behavior, "When Corrections Fail": reported backfire among some ideological subgroups (e.g. on Iraq WMD).
  - Wood & Porter (2019), Political Behavior: more than 10,100 participants across 52 issues; no correction produced backfire. https://www.doi.org/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y
  - Swire-Thompson, DeGutis & Lazer (2020), J Applied Research in Memory and Cognition 9:286-299: review finds worldview and familiarity backfire lack robust support; calls for more reliable measures and better-powered designs. https://networkscienceinstitute.org/publications/searching-for-the-backfire-effect-measurement-and-design-considerations
  - Swire-Thompson et al. (2022), J Exp Psych: General (two longitudinal studies, N=388/532): no item backfired beyond test-retest control; item backfire rates tracked low item reliability (r about -.6 to -.7), pointing to measurement noise. https://networkscienceinstitute.org/publications/backfire-effects-after-correcting-misinformation-are-strongly-associated-with-reliability
  - Ecker, Sharkey & Swire-Thompson (2023), PLOS One: vaccine-myth corrections that repeated the myth reduced misconceptions; no familiarity or fear backfire. https://www.networkscienceinstitute.org/publications/correcting-vaccine-misinformation-a-failure-to-replicate-familiarity-or-fear-driven-backfire-effects
  - Prike, Blackley, Swire-Thompson & Ecker (2023), Cognitive Research (3 expts, N=1,156): standalone corrections did not backfire immediately or after a week; possible backfire only in open-ended answers among people who distrusted the correction. https://www.networkscienceinstitute.org/publications/examining-the-replicability-of-backfire-effects-after-standalone-corrections
  - Ecker et al. (2022), Nature Reviews Psychology: review of misinformation drivers, continued influence, prebunking and debunking. https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/the-psychological-drivers-of-misinformation-belief-and-its-resist/
- Evidence grade: Contested (unchanged). Several independent 2020-2023 studies fail to find worldview or familiarity backfire and tie past findings to unreliable measures. Corrections usually work; distrust of the source is the one candidate boundary condition.
- Design applications:
  - Lead with the fact, mention the myth once with a warning, then restate the fact ("truth sandwich"). Repeating the myth inside a correction is not the hazard it was thought to be. Signal: belief accuracy in pre/post tests.
  - Make the correction's source credible and visible (who says so, why). Distrust is the likeliest route to backfire. Signal: perceived-trust rating on correction UI.
  - Prefer context (related articles, sources) over accusatory labels. Signal: share rate of flagged content.
  - Correct users' mental models in-product with neutral, non-blaming copy. Signal: repeat-error rate.
- Real product examples: Facebook replaced "Disputed" red flags with Related Articles in December 2017 and reported fewer shares. https://about.fb.com/news/2017/12/news-feed-fyi-updates-in-our-fight-against-misinformation/ X/Twitter Community Notes add context rather than labels.
- Enterprise/B2B note: when user research contradicts an executive's assumption, give the finding plus a path forward and cite the evidence source. Corrections land; ego threat and distrust of the messenger are the real risks.
- Ethics/watch-out: do not use "backfire" as an excuse to avoid correcting misinformation. The evidence says corrections usually help.
