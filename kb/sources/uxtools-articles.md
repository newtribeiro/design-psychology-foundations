## 12 Ways to Utilize Other Departments in User Research
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/12-ways-to-utilize-other-departments-in-user-research
- Type: article
- Date/author: Nov 14, 2022 — Taylor Palmer (Founding Designer @ Stable)
- Summary:
  - Other teams (Support, Customer Success, Sales, Training, Marketing) often talk to customers more than designers; partner instead of competing, but the designer owns the integrity/prioritization of insights.
  - 12 tactics: invite Customer Success to sprint planning; customer advisory board (often compensated with discounts/early access/swag); shared feedback repository (Slack → Zapier → board, needs weekly pruning, treat like a database); rotate onto/shadow support ("listening tour"); access recorded/transcribed sales calls; join sales calls and turn walkthroughs into impromptu user tests; on-site client visits (contextual inquiry); add UX questions to CRM post-call forms; new-hire usability tests (before product training); quarterly "voice of customer" decks; copy review with customer education/training teams ("no one calls it that"); centralize outreach in one tool (e.g. Marketo) to avoid spamming customers.
  - Each tactic has a caveat: single data point, loudest-voice bias, sales calls aren't research calls, advisory boards have their own agenda, don't wear participants out.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Add 1–2 UX questions to Sales/CS CRM forms to get a filterable, continuous insight stream.
  - Test prototypes with new hires in their first days, before training contaminates them.
  - Run your product vocabulary past training/support staff; adopt the words customers actually use.
  - Cross-reference any departmental anecdote with another source before acting.
- Psychology links:
  - Survey Bias — advisory boards, sales calls and support tickets are skewed samples (motivated, loudest voices).
  - Availability Heuristic — vivid recent support complaints can be overweighted; the article warns to check representativeness.
  - Survivorship Bias — support only hears from users who reached out; silent churners are missing.
  - Curse of Knowledge — new hires and trainers reveal jargon insiders no longer notice.
  - Reciprocity — advisory boards trade perks (discounts, early access, swag) for ongoing feedback.
  - Mental Model — using customers' own words in copy aligns UI language with how users think.
- New concepts: Customer advisory board — standing group of engaged customers/domain experts consulted regularly; Contextual inquiry — observing users in their real environment; Voice of Customer (VoC) report — periodic synthesis of customer-facing teams' anecdotes vs. roadmap.
- References & resources: Design Tools Survey 2021 (Research Repository section) — https://uxtools.co/survey-2021 ; tools mentioned: Slack, Zapier, Asana, Marketo.

## 17 Tools That Will Streamline Your UX Research
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/17-tools-that-will-streamline-your-ux-research
- Type: tool-category
- Date/author: Oct 13, 2021 — Jordan Bowman (Design Lead @ Moody's)
- Summary:
  - Curated research stack by job: Recruiting (Ethnio, UXtweak recruiting widget/user panel); User testing (Sprig in-product microsurveys & async interviews, Optimal Workshop card sort/tree test/first-click, Useberry prototype tests, Google Optimize A/B, Maze unmoderated tests); Surveys (Google Forms, Tally with answer piping); Interviews (Lookback, Ribbon, Grain clip/share); Analytics (Hotjar heatmaps, session recordings); Synthesis/repository (Dovetail, Condens, Notion, Slab).
  - 2021 prices listed (most have free tiers); tools can be stitched together and upgraded as budget grows.
  - Note: Google Optimize was later discontinued (Sept 2023) — treat as historical.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Use in-product intercepts (Sprig/Ethnio/UXtweak) to ask questions at the key moment rather than in retrospective surveys.
  - Pair quant (Hotjar, A/B) with qual (Lookback/Grain) and keep everything in one repository (Dovetail/Condens/Notion).
  - Use tree testing/card sorting to validate IA before building navigation.
- Psychology links:
  - Survey Bias — tool choice (in-context microsurveys vs. emailed forms) changes response quality and recall bias.
  - Hawthorne Effect — moderated sessions can alter behavior; passive session recordings/heatmaps capture more natural behavior.
  - Mental Model — card sorting/tree testing directly measure users' mental models of content structure.
  - Confirmation Bias — A/B and multivariate tests guard against designers' favored concepts.
- New concepts: Card sorting / tree testing / first-click testing — IA validation methods; Unmoderated usability testing — remote tasks completed without a facilitator.

## 3 ways MagicPath closes the design-to-code gap
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/3-ways-magicpath-closes-the-design-to-code-gap
- Type: article (newsletter)
- Date/author: Jan 28, 2026 — Tommy Geoco (@designertom)
- Summary:
  - MagicPath (by Pietro Schirano) offers three entry points onto one canvas that all output production code: (1) Figma Connect — paste existing Figma files, wire into flows; (2) Web Capture — grab working components from a live site (not screenshots); (3) Prompt — generate UI with real states/navigation, inheriting imported Figma variables or prebuilt design systems.
  - Author's thesis: 2026 is the year "roundtripping" between code and canvas gets good; starting from what's already live, not a blank canvas, is the key shift.
  - Fit: component-thinking product/design-engineering teams; skip if no design system or purely visual exploration.
  - Quick hits: Figma Glass effect controls improved; Kimi K2.5 topped DesignArena; Cursor semantic search.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Feed AI tools your design tokens/variables so output matches your product instead of generic templates.
  - Prototype on top of the shipped product (captured components) to test changes in realistic context.
- Psychology links:
  - Familiarity Bias — designers adopt tools that let them start from existing files/products rather than a new world.
  - Sunk Cost Effect — years of Figma work make import-first tools far more attractive than restart-from-scratch tools.
  - Social Proof — the post cites bookmarks, views and a respected designer's endorsement as adoption signals.
  - Authority Bias — "when Dann Petty is excited, I pay attention" reasoning.
- New concepts: Roundtripping — moving designs back and forth between code and canvas without loss.
- References & resources: MagicPath — https://www.magicpath.ai/ ; DesignArena (AI design model leaderboard) — https://www.designarena.ai/ ; Cursor — https://cursor.com/ ; Tommy Geoco YouTube — https://www.youtube.com/@designertom (newsletter links in source are beehiiv-tracked redirects)

## 33 Activity Ideas for Remote UX Workshops
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/33-activity-ideas-for-remote-ux-workshops
- Type: article (reference list)
- Date/author: Apr 28, 2021 — Jordan Bowman (Design Lead @ Moody's)
- Summary:
  - Workshop = sequence of activities in 4 phases, each with description / when to use / tip.
  - Icebreakers: Zombie Cats (team name from 2 shared traits), Pic + Question, The Aliens Have Landed (explain product in 5 symbols), Two Truths and a Lie, Bucket List.
  - Generating ideas: Reverse Brainstorming (make the problem worse, then invert), Postup, Storyboarding (8 panels/10 min), Crazy 8's, S.C.A.M.P.E.R., Concept Map, Roleplay, Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, Disney Creative Strategy (dreamer → realist → critic, in order), 4-Up/2-Up wireframes, Journey Map, Brainwriting (silent rotating columns; good for introverts), Five Whys, T-Chart.
  - Processing ideas: Kano Model (attractive / must-have / performance / indifferent), Dot Voting (+ red "resistance" dots; weighted dots for decision-maker), NUF test (New/Useful/Feasible 1–7), Affinity Diagram (keep an "Ungrouped" bucket), How-Now-Wow matrix, RICE ((Reach×Impact×Confidence)/Effort), Impact/Effort chart, Assumption Grid (certainty × risk), $100 Test, Importance/Urgency (Eisenhower) chart.
  - Wrap-up: Start/Stop/Continue, Playback, 4 L's (Liked/Learned/Lacked/Longed For), What? So What? Now What?
  - General tips: one clear goal, pre-built board with instructions, everyone must "do" not watch, frame each activity with a question, timebox, take breaks.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Use silent/parallel methods (Brainwriting, Postup, simultaneous dot voting) to avoid groupthink and loudest-voice dominance.
  - Pick a prioritization frame (RICE, Kano, Impact/Effort) BEFORE the session so ranking is less political.
  - Run Disney Strategy strictly in order to separate divergent and convergent thinking.
  - Close every workshop with an action-oriented retro (Start/Stop/Continue) to convert ideas into commitments.
- Psychology links:
  - Bandwagon Effect — simultaneous (not sequential) dot voting and silent brainwriting prevent people copying early opinions.
  - Anchoring Bias — the first idea shared anchors a group; Crazy 8's/4-Up generate many before discussion.
  - Delighters — Kano "attractive" quadrant = delighters; must-haves are expected baseline.
  - Empathy Gap — Roleplay, storyboarding and journey maps force teams into the user's state.
  - Commitment & Consistency — public wrap-up actions (Start/Stop/Continue, Playback) increase follow-through.
  - Decision Fatigue — timeboxing, breaks and few voting options keep group decisions sharp.
- New concepts: Kano model — classifies features by how presence/absence affects satisfaction; RICE — prioritization score (Reach×Impact×Confidence/Effort); S.C.A.M.P.E.R. — 7-lens ideation checklist; Five Whys / Fishbone — root-cause analysis; Disney Creative Strategy — dreamer/realist/critic role sequence; Affinity diagramming — clustering notes by theme; Brainwriting — silent written brainstorming.
- References & resources: NN/g Workshop activities — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/workshop-activities/ ; NN/g Tools for remote UX workshops — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tools-remote-ux-workshops/ ; UX Collective "Comprehensive anatomy of all UX design thinking workshops" — https://uxdesign.cc/comprehensive-anatomy-of-all-ux-design-thinking-workshops-c416706cd3e2 ; UX Tools whiteboarding tools database — https://uxtools.co/tools/whiteboarding ; UX Tools challenges: Wireframe — https://uxtools.co/challenges/wireframe/ , Journey map — https://uxtools.co/challenges/journey-map/

## 5 Principles of Exceptional Case Studies in UX Portfolios
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/5-principles-of-exceptional-case-studies-in-ux-portfolios
- Type: article
- Date/author: Feb 8, 2022 — Taylor Palmer
- Summary:
  - Five principles: be unique (no template; teammates' identical case studies can't be differentiated); keep the audience in mind (recruiters skim ~90 seconds at first); set expectations upfront; tell a story, not a process; answer "why".
  - Treat the portfolio as a design project: scannable headings, highlight what you're proud of; use descriptive headings ("Discovering how much customers care about privacy") instead of generic "Research".
  - Show the end result first, then deconstruct; write a sharp one-sentence lead with outcome metrics (example leads with % lifts given).
  - Answer context questions early: platform, in-house/agency/student, built/released?, team, timeline, role, success metric, outcome.
  - Story mode: stay connected to the problem, connect research insights to design decisions, use annotated before/after images, show constraints and mess, show what you learned. Skip stock-photo personas and whiteboard photos that don't explain impact.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Lead with final design + one-line outcome; add a context fact box.
  - Rewrite every section heading as an insight.
  - Collect assets/metrics during projects, not after.
- Psychology links:
  - Serial Position Effect — reviewers retain the opening (end result, lead) best, so front-load it.
  - Visual Hierarchy — headings/highlights make a 90-second scan productive.
  - Storytelling Effect — narrative of problem → insight → decision is more persuasive than a process checklist.
  - Picture Superiority Effect — annotated before/after images communicate impact faster than text.
  - Selective Attention — skimming reviewers only notice what is emphasized.
  - Halo Effect — a strong first screen colors judgment of the rest of the portfolio.
- References & resources: UX Tools course "Master Your UX Portfolio" — https://uxtools.co/products/master-your-ux-portfolio/ ; Best portfolio builders comparison — https://uxtools.co/compare-tools/best-portfolio-builders ; Fixing User Personas — https://uxtools.co/blog/fixing-user-personas/

## 7 changes in brand world-building
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/7-changes-in-brand-world-building
- Type: article (newsletter + livestream promo)
- Date/author: Sep 3, 2026 — Tommy Geoco
- Summary:
  - Prompted by Runway's Solaris (a world model that streams UI frame by frame): if interfaces are generated rather than coded, brand books must cover new surfaces.
  - Seven shifts: (1) interfaces generated, not coded (Solaris; World Labs Atlas for 3D); (2) portfolios become walkable places (Pablo Stanley, Apple-Maps game); (3) audiences steer the next scene live (Infinite Slop, Glitch) so brand must survive strangers driving it; (4) fast AI animation makes motion timing and emotional duration part of identity; (5) sound is brand — guides usually skip it; (6) mixed media must land in product (site, overlay, thumbnail, film, game easter egg); (7) brand toolkits become tiny free browser tools (Designjoy's Playgrnd, 32 micro-tools).
  - Two buckets: surfaces a model/crowd controls (1–4) vs. surfaces a studio controls but lacks guidelines for (5–7).
  - Livestream panel: Metalab, Smith & Diction (Calendly rebrand), Perplexity, Hoodzpah, Primary, Designjoy, Jamey Gannon.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Extend brand guidelines to motion timing, sonic identity and behavior in generated/interactive contexts, not just static marks and palettes.
  - Define brand invariants that hold when AI or audiences generate the content.
- Psychology links:
  - Sensory Appeal — sound and motion as brand carriers engage more senses and improve recall.
  - Familiarity Bias — consistent cross-surface brand cues keep generated experiences recognizable.
  - Aesthetic-Usability Effect — crafted brand worlds raise perceived quality of the product.
  - Storytelling Effect — "world-building" frames brand as narrative universe rather than style rules.
  - Peak-End Rule — how long an emotion "holds" in motion design shapes the remembered moments.
- New concepts: Brand world-building / brand universe — brand defined as an explorable system across media, not a static guide; Sonic branding — audio identity as a brand asset; World model — generative model simulating an interactive environment/UI in real time.

## 7 Practical Tips for Better Microcopy
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/7-practical-tips-for-better-microcopy
- Type: article (short checklist)
- Date/author: Aug 7, 2020 — Jordan Bowman
- Summary:
  - (1) Use "you" — direct address raises attention; (2) start buttons/important copy with a verb; (3) pre-empt concerns — explain sensitive actions before users worry about motives; (4) natural, jargon-free conversational language; (5) default to active voice; (6) helpful, empathetic, non-technical error messages that explain what happened; (7) write iteratively — test, refine, ship.
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Audit every CTA: verb-first, specific outcome ("Save draft" not "OK").
  - Add reassurance microcopy next to permission/payment/data requests.
  - Rewrite error messages: what happened + how to fix, no blame.
- Psychology links:
  - Signifiers — verb-first labels signal what an interactive element will do.
  - Feedforward — copy that tells users what will happen before they act (preventing concerns).
  - Reactance — unexplained data/permission requests trigger resistance; transparency reduces it.
  - Cognitive Load — plain language and active voice are faster to parse.
  - Curse of Knowledge — jargon and technical error codes come from insiders forgetting users' perspective.
  - Negativity Bias — threatening/negative error wording is disproportionately memorable and harmful.
- New concepts: UX writing / microcopy — small interface text (labels, hints, errors) that guides action.
- References & resources: none beyond author profile.

## A room full of prototypers
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/a-room-full-of-prototypers
- Type: article (newsletter / year-end essay)
- Date/author: Dec 4, 2025 — Tommy Geoco
- Summary:
  - Annual Design Tools Survey 2025 planned to focus on prototyping as the main thread.
  - Observed pattern: designers across freelance → enterprise are moving "closer to the source of truth" (code/production) — examples: Lee Black (3D in Figma, Framer, Midjourney/Runway), MeshTimes (non-coder shipping 5+ apps), Tom Johnson (dithering trend, Basedash → Vercel), Hang Xu (MagicPatterns).
  - Tool landscape: vibe-code tools adding visual inspectors (Dazl, v0, Bolt, Lovable); AI infinite canvases (Paper, MagicPath, Opacity, Figma Make); Webflow/Framer AI; node-based gen-media tools (FLORA, Weavy — acquired by Figma ~4 months after launch).
  - Three stances on AI: optimism, pessimism ("real work is research/strategy/systems"), pragmatism (adapt, keep what works). Author picks optimistic pragmatism.
  - Sponsor note: Granola AI notepad; insight that a team's true source of truth is its conversations.
  - Docu-series ep. 2 on Soren Iverson's agency (viral absurdity for brands like Duolingo).
- Actionable takeaways:
  - Experiment publicly with prototyping tools; keep only what improves your workflow.
  - Move design artifacts closer to production (code prototypes) to reduce handoff loss.
- Psychology links:
  - Bandwagon Effect — waves of designers adopting the same AI tools and visual trends (e.g. dithering).
  - Law of the Instrument — risk of tool obsession; the "pessimist" stance warns tools aren't the work.
  - Familiarity Bias — Granola's "good design is invisible" framing; tools succeed when they fit existing habits.
  - Backfire Effect — polarized optimist/pessimist camps entrench rather than update views.
- New concepts: Vibe coding — building software by prompting AI rather than hand-writing code.
- References & resources: links in source are beehiiv-tracked; named tools: Granola, Dazl, v0, Bolt, Lovable, Paper, MagicPath, Opacity, Figma Make, Webflow, Framer, FLORA, Weavy, MagicPatterns, Midjourney, Cursor; Soleio's "The Keeper" (essay); Shopify BFCM site.

- Summary:

  - Anchoring Bias — a proposed plan anchors the group's timeline discussion around design-friendly constraints.
  - IKEA Effect — involving stakeholders early makes them value and defend the project they helped shape.
  - Commitment & Consistency — publicly agreed roles, timelines and action items increase follow-through.
  - Planning Fallacy — explicitly asking "what have we overlooked in this timeline?" counters optimistic estimates.
  - Reactance — handling resistance privately avoids triggering defensiveness in a group setting.

## Stochastic vs. Deterministic Design
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/stochastic-vs-deterministic-design

- Summary:
  - Frames a fork in AI-era design tooling into three bets about design labour:

- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Encode your design system as a machine-readable rule file (tokens, accessibility, usage) agents can validate against.
  - Use agents for breadth (variants), reserve human judgment for final selection.
  - Evaluate tools by which labour model they assume, not by feature lists.

  - Mental Model — stochastic, per-user UIs risk breaking users' learned expectations of a stable layout.

## The How (and Why) of User Flows
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/the-how-(and-why)-of-user-flows
- Type: article
- Date/author (if known): Jordan Bowman
- Summary:
  - User flow = series of steps a user takes to achieve a meaningful goal; shown as a flowchart with title, wireframes and notes.
  - Benefits: keeps the user in the conversation, centres work on tasks not pages, foundation for IA/UI/usability testing, exposes dead ends and pain points, improves team communication.
  - Method: gather prelim info (based on research/persona) → title it by user goal → one task, one direction (split if it branches) → draw each step (wireframes preferred) → one-line explanation per step → evaluate and improve.
  - Evaluation questions: Can it be more efficient (fewer steps without crowded screens)? More clear (where am I, what next, did I finish)? Where is friction/doubt? Does it match expectations? Confirm with A/B tests.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Map one complete task per flow, titled with the user's goal.
  - Use low-fi wireframes in flows to keep discussion on the path, not pixel details.
  - Audit each flow for step count, clarity of progress, completion signal and friction points.
- Psychology links:
  - Mental Model — flows are checked against what users expect to happen next.
  - Goal Gradient Effect — clear progress and fewer steps keep users motivated toward completion.
  - Cognitive Load — reducing steps and distractions lowers effort per task.
  - Storytelling Effect — flows "tell a story" which aids team comprehension.
  - Empathy Gap — writing from the user's perspective counters designers' drift into requirements and jargon.
  - Feedback Loop — a clear completion signal confirms the task is done.
- New concepts: User flow — goal-based step diagram of a task; User persona — research-based archetype of a target user.

- Summary:
  - Users aren't irrational; they rely on predictable subconscious shortcuts (heuristics), which is rational online because errors are cheap (back button), clicking is rewarding, and pages are complex.

  - Consider the post-click experience: strong information scent in link labels, graceful recovery from wrong clicks.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Count decisions on a key page and cut them.
  - Pre-populate form fields with the most common value.
  - Limit meaningful colours to ≤4 and reserve animation for purposeful moments.
  - Write descriptive link labels so the destination is predictable.
- Psychology links:
  - Hick's Law — explicitly cited; fewer choices per page shorten decision time (DPP).
  - Loss Aversion — explicitly cited; losses weigh about twice gains.
  - Availability Heuristic — users overweight what's visible/recalled immediately.

  - Visual Hierarchy — size, contrast, whitespace signal priority for scanners.

## Translating User Research Into Design
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/translating-user-research-into-design
- Type: article
- Date/author (if known): Taylor Palmer
- Summary:
  - Research that never reaches designs is "performative"; start each study with a goal/hypothesis so next steps are obvious (confirm → proceed; disprove → next hypothesis).
  - Some studies (concept tests) lead straight to design; others (market studies, mental models) have a longer arc — don't neglect them.
  - Synthesis turns raw artifacts into human language (storyboards, sketches, graphs, bullets); participants rarely name problems ("your IA is confusing") — read between the lines. Rolling synthesis after each session.
  - Share and align: manage stakeholder expectations; prioritise using a multi-dimensional framework (not just complexity or urgency — also scope, cost, pervasiveness); share insights broadly to find allies and past attempts.
  - "Design glue": every solution should trace back to an insight; test by critiquing with someone unfamiliar with the study.
  - Find opportunities by grouping insights by app location, journey stage, or user type; sketch after each session; prototype if confidence is low; testing is another research round.
  - Solutions needn't be UI — content strategy, branding, backend, market changes.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Write a hypothesis before research and decide what confirm/disprove means.
  - Annotate designs with the research insight each decision answers ("design glue").
  - Cluster insights by journey stage to spot hotspots.
  - Use a prioritisation framework with several dimensions.
- Psychology links:
  - Confirmation Bias — critique with outsiders checks whether ideas come from research or personal preference.
  - Mental Model — some studies target users' mental models with a longer path to UI.
  - Storytelling Effect — storyboards and narratives make insights persuasive to stakeholders.
  - Chunking — clustering insights by location/journey/user type makes them actionable.
  - Survivorship Bias — research not converted to design leaves only visible UI ideas considered.
- New concepts: Design glue — explicit trace from each design decision to a research insight; Rolling synthesis — synthesising continuously after each session; User journey — stages a user goes through across touchpoints.

## Usability Testing in 4 Simplified Steps
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/usability-testing-in-4-simplified-steps
- Type: article
- Date/author (if known): Jordan Bowman
- Summary:
  - (1) Clear goal: 2–3 realistic tasks — not too vague ("explore") nor too prescriptive (step-by-step); e.g. "buy shoes under $40".
  - (2) Recruit 3–5 representative participants (diminishing returns beyond); avoid colleagues/friends (biased); source from user base, support/social channels, forums; ≤15 min tests often free, or offer product credit/gift cards; schedule via Calendly.
  - (3) Run it: clickable prototype (Marvel/InVision), consistent script, record (Zoom/Meet); relax participant ("we're testing the site, not you"), think-aloud, give real-life scenario, stay neutral and silent, deflect with "What do you think?"; debrief with open questions.
  - (4) Analyse: success/efficiency per task, blockers, patterns across participants; rank issues and solutions.
  - Any testing beats none.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Phrase tasks as goals, not instructions.
  - Test with 5 users, iterate, test again.
  - Script sessions and respond to questions with neutral prompts.
- Psychology links:
  - Observer-Expectancy Effect — neutral, silent moderation and scripts avoid steering participants.
  - Hawthorne Effect — being watched changes behaviour; reassuring participants reduces it.
  - Self-Serving Bias — participants blame themselves; telling them it's the software's fault elicits honest behaviour.
  - Pareto Principle — 5 users uncover most issues; more yields diminishing returns.
  - Confirmation Bias — avoiding internal staff/friends prevents biased validation.
- New concepts: Think-aloud protocol — participants verbalise thoughts while doing tasks; Moderated vs unmoderated testing — with or without a live facilitator.

  - Law of Proximity — mega menus' grouping clusters related links.
  - Discoverability — hidden menus reduce discovery of key sections.

  - Law of the Instrument — rigidly applying one process to every project is "when all you have is a hammer".

  - Fey (Thiago Costa): interfaces lit like candlelight; studies how light wraps glass/metal/skin; Nomad Sculpt for form, Figma vectors, rebuilds depth in CSS; avoids referencing other apps to avoid subconscious copying; inspired by Caravaggio and the game Inside.
  - 10/42 Studio (Lee Black): cinematic Figma files with gradient stacks, gifs; treats interaction like film scoring ("sometimes it needs to pause"); bends/breaks tools deliberately.
  - Craft framed as the last unfair advantage in an AI-mandated world.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Constrain features and differentiate through emotional design (sound, motion, pacing).
  - Draw inspiration from non-software media (film, architecture, games, packaging).
  - Prototype physically or in materials to test feel before deciding.
- Psychology links:
  - Sensory Appeal — sound, light and material texture create memorable multi-sensory experiences.
  - Delighters — unexpected emotional touches make utilities memorable.
  - Von Restorff Effect — strange, distinctive apps stand out amid sameness.
  - Skeuomorphism — light and material realism (not nostalgia) gives physical weight to UI.
  - Aesthetic-Usability Effect — beautiful craft increases perceived quality and tolerance.
  - Affect Heuristic — emotional resonance drives preference and memory more than utility.
- New concepts: Defiant craft — deliberately prioritising emotional depth over best-practice utility; Emotional design — designing for feeling (Don Norman's visceral/behavioural/reflective levels).

## Your UI needs more Walt Disney
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/blog/your-ui-needs-more-walt-disney
- Type: article (newsletter)
- Date/author (if known): 2025; Tommy Geoco; on Rauno Freiberg (Staff Design Engineer, Vercel) and his course Devouring Details
- Summary:
  - "Design with the material": skip wireframes/mockups; prototype interactions directly in code, since code's constraints reveal realistic behaviour and "happy accidents".
  - Build fast with throwaway "spaghetti code"; snapshot iterations as video/snippets; share with peers early.
  - Ruthless validation as filtering: push ideas to absurd extremes (dial to 100) so you can see that a simpler earlier version felt better.
  - Robustness: core interactions (scroll, text input, navigation) must work 100% — test like QA (rapid clicks, fast scrolling, low battery, flaky network); an interaction that works 80% of the time breaks perceived quality (e.g. glitchy custom text cursor).
  - Disney principle "Follow-Through and Overlapping Action": stagger secondary elements 100–200 ms after the main transition; simultaneous motion reads mechanical, sequential reads organic.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Prototype key interactions in code early (even messy).
  - Stress-test every interaction under adverse conditions before polishing visuals.
  - Add 100–200 ms staggered delays to secondary elements in transitions.
  - Explore extremes to calibrate, then pull back to the simplest version that feels right.
- Psychology links:
  - Negativity Bias — one glitch outweighs many smooth interactions in perceived quality and trust.
  - Aesthetic-Usability Effect — choreographed motion raises perceived polish and intentionality.
  - Feedback Loop — rapid iteration and peer review of interaction snapshots.
  - Contrast — exploring extremes makes the better, simpler option obvious by comparison.
  - Law of Prägnanz — staggered, organic motion is perceived as a coherent, natural whole.
  - Chronoception — 100–200 ms delays shape the perceived timing and smoothness of transitions.
- New concepts: Disney's 12 principles of animation — classic animation rules (e.g. follow-through and overlapping action, easing) applied to UI motion; Design engineer — designer who works directly in code; Robustness — interactions that never fail under real-world stress.
