# Batch C — uxtools.co Challenges, Tools Database & misc pages

## Batch overview
- Scope: 42 non-blog/episode/survey pages. That is the /challenges index, **18** challenge pages (the index lists 18, not 19; no 19th key exists in `window.__ux`), 9 /tools categories, about, community, newsletter, design, design-system, bundle, bundle/terms, detach-2026, summer-bundle-2026, home, plus privacy-terms and thank-you. The /blog and /episodes index pages are just lists of posts and episodes that batches A and B already cover, so they are not summarised here.
- The challenges follow a Double Diamond-like sequence: Understand (5), Ideate (3), Test (5), Implement (5). Each one gives a realistic scenario and competitors, a task, an extra-credit stretch, 4 hand-picked tutorials (always ending with a YouTube video) and suggested tools. Together they work as a ready-made practice curriculum for the skill.
- Research challenges (interview, persona, empathy map, journey map, diary study, survey, usability test, card sort) are where the **bias-control** principles matter most: Survey Bias, Observer-Expectancy Effect, Confirmation Bias, Hawthorne Effect, False Consensus Effect, Curse of Knowledge, Empathy Gap, Hindsight Bias.
- Build challenges (form, onboarding, IA, wireframe, prototype, design system, user flow) depend mostly on **load and attention** principles: Cognitive Load, Hick's Law, Progressive Disclosure, Mental Model, Recognition Over Recall, Visual Hierarchy, Signifiers, Feedback Loop, Goal Gradient Effect, Aha! Moment.
- The Tools Database shows survey-based usage share and ratings. Figma dominates every craft category: UI 82.3%, basic prototyping 71.4%, design systems 59.2%, whiteboarding through FigJam 48.8%. Research tooling is fragmented, with no tool above ~13%. These are live examples of Familiarity Bias, Bandwagon Effect, Default Bias and Social Proof in tool adoption.
- The commercial pages (bundle, summer bundle, Detach perks, newsletter) show textbook persuasion patterns: Scarcity (1,000 only, countdown), Anchoring Bias ("value" vs $129), Unit Bias / Pseudo-Set Framing (a "bundle of 10"), Social Proof (testimonials, 100K+), Authority Bias (Rising Star award, named design leads) and Loss Aversion ("claim before they disappear").
- Downloads, templates and media found: UX Tools Media Kit 2025 (Figma Slides deck), Stephanie Walter's user journey map PDF templates, Design Systems Handbook (DesignBetter, free book), the A11y Project checklist, two home-page hero MP4 loops, and about 18 tutorial YouTube videos (listed under each challenge). There are no downloadable challenge templates or Figma files on uxtools.co itself. The "Figma" media entries are only tool homepage links.

## Challenge: User Interview (Understand)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/user-interview
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a product designer at a startup building a note-taking app (competitors Notion, Evernote) needs to understand future users' current workflows.
  - Research questions: what people use digital notes for, their current process, pain points, and how they feel about their current options.
  - Task: plan and run 3 real interviews, extract specific insights that inform features and design, analyse them and give recommendations.
  - Extra credit: 5 or more interviews.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Write a discussion guide built around past behaviour ("tell me about the last time…") rather than hypotheticals.
  - Prepare follow-up probes in advance (see the Walter cheatsheet). Synthesise with affinity clustering in Dovetail or Miro.
- Psychology links:
  - Observer-Expectancy Effect — the interviewer's cues and leading questions bias the answers, so stay neutral.
  - Confirmation Bias — designers hear what supports their feature idea, so log disconfirming quotes too.
  - Survey Bias — people say what is socially acceptable. Ask about real past actions instead.
  - Empathy Gap — interviewing in calm conditions underestimates how users act when rushed or stressed.
  - Hindsight Bias — participants rationalise their past workflow after the fact. Probe for concrete artifacts.
  - False Consensus Effect — the reason to interview at all is that designers assume users think like them.
- New concepts: Jobs-to-be-Done — users "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. Interviews should surface the job, not feature requests.
- References & resources:
  - User Interviews: How, When, and Why to Conduct Them (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/
  - A Cheatsheet for User Interview and Follow Ups Questions (Stephanie Walter) — https://stephaniewalter.design/blog/a-cheatsheet-for-user-interview-and-follow-ups-questions/
  - 12 Ways To Improve User Interview Questions (Smashing) — https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2020/06/user-interview-questions/
  - Video: Questions to Ask When You Conduct a User Research Interview — https://youtu.be/auQCdrRfYas
  - Tools: Miro https://miro.com/ ; Notion https://notion.so/ ; Dovetail https://dovetail.com/ ; Zoom https://www.zoom.com/
  - Competitors: Notion, Evernote (archived)

## Challenge: Journey Map (Understand)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/journey-map
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a freelancer researching an airline flight-deals site (competitors Kayak, Expedia), mapping how people find and secure a discount flight.
  - The goal is to surface pain points, opportunities and touch points.
  - Task: build a journey map of buying a flight through a deals app, informed by interviews or diary studies.
  - Extra credit: 2–3 specific design recommendations drawn from the map.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Add an emotion curve. Mark the lowest point and the final moment explicitly, because those dominate how the experience is remembered.
  - Base each stage on research evidence, not assumptions. Tag each opportunity to a stage.
- Psychology links:
  - Peak-End Rule — users judge the trip-booking experience by its worst or best moment and its ending, so the map should flag both.
  - Negativity Bias — pain points such as price changes and hidden fees weigh more than positives.
  - Loss Aversion — fear of missing a deal or of the price rising drives booking anxiety.
  - Scarcity — "only 2 seats left" style cues are a key touch point to evaluate ethically.
  - Hyperbolic Discounting — the gap between a deal now and a better deal later explains hesitation and abandonment.
  - Storytelling Effect — a journey map persuades stakeholders because it is a narrative.
- New concepts: Service blueprint — an extension of the journey map that adds backstage processes. Touchpoint — any point where the user meets the service.
- References & resources:
  - Journey Mapping 101 (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/
  - 5 Stages of Successful Customer Journey Maps (dscout) — https://dscout.com/people-nerds/customer-journey-maps-5-stages
  - An Introduction to User Journey Map + **PDF templates (download)** (Stephanie Walter) — https://stephaniewalter.design/blog/an-introduction-to-user-journey-map-pdf-templates/
  - Video: Customer Journey Map Workshop — https://youtu.be/X7iXcP-wIkk
  - Tools: Miro, Figma

## Challenge: Competitive Analysis (Understand)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/competitive-analysis
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a large financial firm is building a commission-free stock-trading mobile app, and the designer must review the competitive landscape early.
  - The goal is to find opportunities to stand out and differentiate.
  - Task: run a UX competitive analysis of the real competitors and write a report.
  - Extra credit: concrete differentiation recommendations and design opportunities.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Compare the same tasks across competitors (onboarding, first trade, portfolio view) using a feature-and-heuristic matrix.
  - Separate "table stakes" patterns (copy these for familiarity) from differentiators.
- Psychology links:
  - Familiarity Bias — users carry expectations from the incumbent apps, so match the conventions.
  - Mental Model — competitors define the model users already hold for "buying a stock".
  - Survivorship Bias — you only see the apps that survived, not the patterns that failed.
  - Noble Edge Effect — differentiating through visible ethical stances, such as transparent fees, can be an advantage.
  - Variable Reward / Cashless Effect — trading apps' gamified confetti and frictionless buying are ethically loaded patterns to audit.
- New concepts: Jakob's Law — users spend most of their time on other products and expect yours to work the same way. Kano model — splits features into basic, performance and delighter.
- References & resources:
  - How to Conduct a Competitive Audit (dscout) — https://dscout.com/people-nerds/conduct-a-competitive-audit
  - Essential Ingredients For Every Competitive Analysis (UX Collective) — https://uxdesign.cc/essential-ingredients-for-every-competitive-analysis-ce08906a4079
  - A Guide to Competitive Analysis for UX Design (Adobe XD Ideas) — https://xd.adobe.com/ideas/process/user-research/guide-to-competitive-analysis-ux-design/
  - Competitive Analysis Done Right (UX Collective) — https://uxdesign.cc/ux-competitive-analysis-done-right-f02ea3953294

## Challenge: User Persona (Understand)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/user-persona
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: the first design hire at a young real-estate site (competitors Zillow, Trulia) that has never done user research.
  - Key principle: personas must come from research, not assumptions.
  - Task: research people who are or were house-hunting (interviews, diary studies, surveys), let persona types emerge, and build one persona.
  - Extra credit: a second, contrasting persona.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Anchor each persona attribute to evidence (quotes, observed behaviour). Leave out decorative demographics.
  - Present personas as tools that make users memorable to the team (NN/g).
- Psychology links:
  - False Consensus Effect — personas counter the team's tendency to design for themselves.
  - Barnum-Forer Effect — vague persona traits feel accurate to everyone and are useless, so be specific.
  - Storytelling Effect — a narrative persona is remembered and used by the team.
  - Picture Superiority Effect — a face and a visual layout make the persona stick in team memory.
  - Confirmation Bias — assumption-based personas just encode existing beliefs.
- New concepts: Proto-persona — an assumption-based draft to be validated later. JTBD persona — defined by the situation and motivation rather than demographics.
- References & resources:
  - Fixing User Personas (uxtools.co) — https://uxtools.co/blog/fixing-user-personas/
  - A 5 Step Framework for Building Better Personas (dscout) — https://dscout.com/people-nerds/designing-better-personas
  - Personas Make Users Memorable for Product Team Members (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona/
  - Video: Tips for How to Create & Use Personas — https://youtu.be/7z7893qE_0w
  - Tools: Miro, Notion, Figma

## Challenge: Empathy Map (Understand)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/empathy-map
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: the sole designer at a video-call company (competitor Zoom). The team has only vague ideas about its users.
  - The goal is to visualise user attitudes and behaviours and share them with teammates.
  - Task: choose a specific user type (for example a young person calling extended family, a salesperson meeting clients, or a remote worker) and build an empathy map backed by interviews, diaries or surveys.
  - Extra credit: a second empathy map for another user type.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Use the 4 quadrants (Says / Thinks / Does / Feels). Contradictions between Says and Does are where the insights are.
  - Keep one map per user segment. Never merge segments.
- Psychology links:
  - Empathy Gap — the method exists to bridge the gap between the designer's state and the user's state.
  - Cognitive Dissonance — gaps between what users say and what they do expose dissonance worth designing for.
  - Curse of Knowledge — insiders can't imagine a novice's confusion. The map forces their perspective.
  - Affect Heuristic — the Feels quadrant captures emotions such as camera anxiety that drive decisions.
  - Spotlight Effect — on video calls, users overestimate how much others notice them, a real driver of feature needs like filters and self-view hiding.
- New concepts: Say/Do gap — the difference between reported and actual behaviour.
- References & resources:
  - Empathy Mapping: The First Step in Design Thinking (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empathy-mapping/
  - 10 Tips to Develop Better Empathy Maps (Adobe XD Ideas) — https://xd.adobe.com/ideas/process/user-research/10-tips-develop-better-empathy-maps/
  - Empathy Mapping: A Guide to Getting Inside a User's Head (UXBooth) — https://www.uxbooth.com/articles/empathy-mapping-a-guide-to-getting-inside-a-users-head/
  - Video: What is an Empathy Map? — https://youtu.be/QwF9a56WFWA
  - Tools: Miro, Figma, Notion

## Challenge: User Flow (Ideate)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/user-flow
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: an early-stage grocery-delivery app startup (competitor Instacart).
  - Task: one user flow covering the whole order: pick a nearby store, add products to the cart, check out, choose a delivery time.
  - Extra credit: a second flow for adding a payment method or re-ordering a past order.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Map decision points and error and edge states, not only the happy path. Count the steps to checkout.
  - Re-order is a key retention flow, so make it a single path that relies on recognition.
- Psychology links:
  - Goal Gradient Effect — visible checkout progress speeds completion.
  - Hick's Law — limit choices at each node (store, slot) to cut decision time.
  - Default Bias — preselect the nearest store and the soonest slot.
  - Recognition Over Recall — re-order from history instead of re-searching.
  - Tesler's Law — the complexity of delivery slots and substitutions must be absorbed by the system, not the user.
  - Provide Exit Points — let users save the cart and leave without losing progress.
- References & resources:
  - The How (and Why) of User Flows (uxtools.co) — https://uxtools.co/blog/the-how-and-why-of-user-flows/
  - Complete Guide to User Flows (Justinmind) — https://www.justinmind.com/blog/user-flow/
  - Flow Design Processes (IxDF) — https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/article/flow-design-processes-focusing-on-the-user-s-needs
  - Video: User Flow Tutorial — https://youtu.be/TIV1y11xz7k
  - Tools: Figma, Miro, Overflow https://overflow.io/

## Challenge: Wireframe (Ideate)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/wireframe
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a freelancer for a new mobile maps app (competitors Google Maps, Waze, Apple Maps) exploring layouts to show stakeholders and users.
  - Task: 3 annotated wireframes: (1) home on open, (2) searching a location, (3) real-time driving directions.
  - Extra credit: a place-detail view and saving a location to favourites.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Annotate the reasoning behind every element. Keep the fidelity low so feedback focuses on structure.
  - The driving screen needs glanceability: large targets and minimal text.
- Psychology links:
  - Fitts's Law — drivers need big, reachable touch targets.
  - Selective Attention / Cognitive Load — while driving, attention is scarce, so show only the next manoeuvre.
  - Visual Hierarchy — the next turn and the ETA should dominate the directions screen.
  - Familiarity Bias — users expect the established map conventions (search bar on top, blue dot).
  - Progressive Disclosure — place details expand from a bottom sheet instead of separate pages.

## Challenge: Digital Prototype (Ideate)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/digital-prototype
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: an educational app (competitors Skillshare, Udemy) designing how users move through several lessons in a course.
  - The goal is to test the "feel": interaction, UI components, typography and image quality.
  - Task: a high-fidelity prototype of watching several video and text lessons in a row. Starting from a wireframe is suggested.
  - Extra credit: add interactivity so viewers get a realistic feel.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Prototype the transition between lessons (autoplay, "next up", completion state). That is where momentum is won or lost.
  - Use ProtoPie or code when Figma interactions can't convey timing and motion.
- Psychology links:
  - Goal Gradient Effect — course progress bars and "2 lessons left" accelerate completion.
  - Zeigarnik Effect — unfinished lessons and "resume" prompts drive return visits.
  - Flow State — reduce friction between lessons (autoplay, remembered position) to keep learners immersed.
  - Feedback Loop — completion checkmarks confirm progress.
  - Aesthetic-Usability Effect — high-fidelity polish makes testers more forgiving, so treat their ratings with care.
  - Peak-End Rule — end each lesson or course with a satisfying completion moment.

## Challenge: Usability Test (Test)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/usability-test
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a restaurant-reservation app (examples OpenTable, Resy, Yelp, Allset, Tock) analysing the key tasks of new users.
  - Task: test the "make a new reservation" flow with 2–3 first-time participants and write a findings report.
  - Ethics and privacy note: give participants a fake or prepaid card, or stop before real payment.
  - Extra credit: 5 or more participants.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Write scenario-based tasks without UI words. Use think-aloud. Don't help participants.
  - Rate findings by severity and frequency. Separate observations from interpretations.
- Psychology links:
  - Hawthorne Effect — being watched changes behaviour, so make the session relaxed and stress that you are testing the product, not them.
  - Observer-Expectancy Effect — the moderator's hints and nods steer participants.
  - Curse of Knowledge — the reason to test with first-time users: designers can't see their own jargon.
  - Self-Serving Bias — participants blame themselves for failures, so reassure them and look past self-blame.
  - Survey Bias — post-task ratings skew positive. Weight observed behaviour over stated satisfaction.
  - Pareto Principle — a few issues cause most failures, and 5 users find most of them.
- New concepts: Think-aloud protocol — participants narrate their thoughts while performing tasks. Severity rating — a ranking of issues by impact × frequency × persistence.
- References & resources:
  - Usability Testing in 4 Simplified Steps (uxtools.co) — https://uxtools.co/blog/usability-testing-in-4-simplified-steps/
  - Usability Testing 101 (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/
  - A Beginner's Guide to User & Usability Testing (Hotjar) — https://www.hotjar.com/usability-testing/ (the source link has a stray `%22` appended)
  - Video: UX Tea Break: What Makes a Good Usability Test? — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2Au2NoSSg-A
  - Tools: Zoom, Lookback https://www.lookback.com/, Maze https://maze.co/, Useberry https://www.useberry.com/, Miro

## Challenge: Card Sorting (Test)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/card-sorting
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: an agency improving an outdoor-gear deals e-commerce site (competitors The Clymb, GearScan, Steep & Cheap). Customers can't find things because the content is organised around company logic rather than user logic.
  - Task: list the pages and content types, run a card sort with 5–10 real people, and report findings to feed a new IA.
  - Extra credit: debrief participants on their reasoning, and/or 15+ participants.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Use an open sort to discover categories and a closed sort or tree test to validate them. Analyse with a similarity matrix.
  - The debrief explains why items were grouped together, which is as valuable as the clusters.
- Psychology links:
  - Mental Model — card sorting exists to expose users' mental models (the NN/g title says so explicitly).
  - Curse of Knowledge — company-centric taxonomy is a classic symptom.
  - Law of Similarity — users group items that feel alike, and labels should reflect that.
  - Chunking — the categories users produce are their natural chunks.
  - Miller's Law — keep top-level categories manageable.
  - False Consensus Effect — the team's assumed groupings rarely match the users'.
- New concepts: Open vs closed card sort — users either create the categories or sort into predefined ones. Tree testing — validating findability in a text-only hierarchy.
- References & resources:
  - Card Sorting 101 (Optimal Workshop) — https://www.optimalworkshop.com/learn/101s/card-sorting/
  - How to Conduct An Effective Card Sort: A Comprehensive Guide (dscout) — https://dscout.com/people-nerds/card-sorting
  - Card Sorting: Uncover Users' Mental Models for Better Information Architecture (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-definition/
  - Video: What's Card Sorting in User Experience Research? — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=25RqpGJ4tJ8
  - Tools: Miro, Trello https://trello.com/, Optimal Workshop https://www.optimalworkshop.com/, Useberry, Pen and Paper (Sneakpeekit)

## Challenge: Survey (Test)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/survey
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a project-management and collaboration app (examples monday.com, Taskade, ClickUp, Basecamp, Flow, Asana). A feature seems hard for new users to learn.
  - The survey is positioned as direction-setting before deeper qualitative work such as journey maps and usability tests.
  - Task: pick a confusing feature (task management, timelines, app structure, team comms or mobile), write and run a survey of new users, and report the findings.
  - Extra credit: record the reasoning behind each question, the survey length, why you chose the tool, and the sample size.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Use neutral wording, one concept per question and balanced scales. Put screeners first and keep it short.
  - Use surveys to find where problems are. Use qualitative methods to understand why.
- Psychology links:
  - Survey Bias — question wording, order and social desirability distort answers.
  - Framing — "How easy was…" versus "How difficult was…" yields different results.
  - Anchoring Bias — early questions and scale endpoints anchor later answers.
  - Decision Fatigue — long surveys degrade answer quality toward the end.
  - Serial Position Effect — options at the start and end of lists get chosen more, so randomise them.
  - Default Bias — a pre-selected answer biases the response.
- New concepts: Acquiescence bias — the tendency to agree with statements, which counts as a form of Survey Bias.
- References & resources:
  - How to Write (Better) Survey Questions (dscout) — https://dscout.com/people-nerds/how-to-write-better-survey-questions
  - 28 Tips for Creating Great Qualitative Surveys (NN/g) — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/qualitative-surveys/
  - This is All You Need to Know to Conduct a UX Survey (UX Planet) — https://uxplanet.org/this-is-all-you-need-to-know-to-conduct-a-ux-survey-50400af45920
  - Video: Step-by-Step Guide to Create an Effective User Research Survey — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=exyHMFVrZCg
  - Tools: Google Forms https://www.google.com/forms/about/, Typeform https://www.typeform.com/, Optimal Workshop Questions https://www.optimalworkshop.com/questions/, Airtable

## Challenge: Heuristic Evaluation (Test)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/heuristic-evaluation
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a designer at Goodreads (or a similar reading-log app). Alongside usability testing, the lead wants an in-house expert review.
  - Task: a heuristic evaluation of the "log a book you've read" flow, with problems and recommendations.
  - Extra credit: also evaluate "save books to read later".
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Use Nielsen's 10 heuristics with 3–5 evaluators working independently, then merge the findings with severity ratings.
  - Pair each issue with the heuristic it breaks, a screenshot and a fix.
- Psychology links:
  - Feedback Loop — "visibility of system status": does logging a book confirm success?
  - Recognition Over Recall — a Nielsen heuristic in its own right. Shelves and recent books should be visible.
  - Mental Model — "match between system and real world" (shelves, read/want-to-read).
  - Signifiers — are rating and shelving controls recognisable as interactive?
  - Confirmation Bias — expert reviewers find what they expect, which is why multiple independent evaluators are used.
  - Zeigarnik Effect — reading goals and "currently reading" exploit open loops. Evaluate whether this is helpful or nagging.
- New concepts: Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics — a standard checklist for expert review.
- References & resources:
  - What Is Heuristic Evaluation and How Does It Improve Your Product? (Adam Fard) — https://adamfard.com/blog/heuristic-evaluation
  - Heuristic Analysis for UX: How to Run a Usability Evaluation (UX Collective) — https://uxdesign.cc/heuristic-analysis-for-ux-how-to-run-a-usability-evaluation-12c86d43936f

## Challenge: Diary Study (Test)
- URL: https://www.uxtools.co/challenges/diary-study
- Type: challenge
- Summary:
  - Scenario: a streaming company (Netflix, Disney+, Amazon Video, Hulu) that wants to see how users' relationship with the product changes over time: changed usage, new discoveries, shifting perceptions.
  - Extra questions: time of day, sharing behaviour, and motivations to open the app.
  - Task: a diary study with 3–5 users and a findings report.
  - Extra credit: 8 or more participants.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:

# Part 4 — Challenge → psychology playbook

Practice material: for each challenge, the principles to apply deliberately and why. Bias-control principles protect the research. Design-lever principles shape the artifact.

| # | Challenge | Apply these principles | Why / how |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **User Interview** | Observer-Expectancy Effect; Confirmation Bias; Survey Bias; Hindsight Bias; Empathy Gap; False Consensus Effect | Ask neutral, past-behaviour questions ("walk me through the last note you took"), and don't react to answers. Log quotes that disconfirm your idea. Ask for real artifacts (their notes app) to get past rationalised memories. Interview users in real contexts to close the empathy gap. Treat every "I'd use that" as weak evidence. |
| 2 | **Journey Map** | Peak-End Rule; Negativity Bias; Loss Aversion; Hyperbolic Discounting; Storytelling Effect; Scarcity | Plot an emotion curve and highlight the lowest point and the end (confirmation and check-in). Weight pain points above delights. Mark where fear of losing a deal causes stress, and where "book later" causes drop-off. Present the map as a story to win stakeholders. Audit urgency cues for dark-pattern risk. |
| 3 | **Competitive Analysis** | Familiarity Bias; Mental Model; Survivorship Bias; Noble Edge Effect; Variable Reward; Cashless Effect | Keep conventions users already learned from incumbents (Jakob's Law). Remember you only see the survivors. Differentiate ethically (fee transparency, anti-gamification) instead of copying confetti rewards and frictionless trading that encourage overtrading. |
| 4 | **User Persona** | False Consensus Effect; Barnum-Forer Effect; Confirmation Bias; Storytelling Effect; Picture Superiority Effect | Ground every trait in research data to beat both "we are the user" and vague horoscope-like traits. Make the persona memorable with narrative and visuals so the team actually uses it. |
| 5 | **Empathy Map** | Empathy Gap; Cognitive Dissonance; Curse of Knowledge; Affect Heuristic; Spotlight Effect | Use the Says vs Does mismatch to find dissonance. Capture feelings (camera self-consciousness, fatigue) that drive decisions more than features do. Keep insider jargon out of the map. |
| 6 | **User Flow** | Goal Gradient Effect; Hick's Law; Default Bias; Recognition Over Recall; Tesler's Law; Provide Exit Points | Show checkout progress. Keep choices at each node minimal. Preselect the nearest store and the soonest slot. Offer one-tap re-order from history. Let the system handle substitutions and slot complexity. Let users save the cart and leave. |
| 7 | **Wireframe** | Fitts's Law; Selective Attention; Cognitive Load; Visual Hierarchy; Familiarity Bias; Progressive Disclosure | Use large targets and minimal text on the driving screen. Make the next manoeuvre dominant. Follow map conventions (search bar on top, location dot). Use a bottom sheet that expands to place details. |
| 8 | **Digital Prototype** | Goal Gradient Effect; Zeigarnik Effect; Flow State; Feedback Loop; Peak-End Rule; Aesthetic-Usability Effect | Show course progress and "resume where you left off". Make lesson-to-lesson transitions smooth (autoplay with a countdown). Add completion checkmarks and a celebratory course end. Remember that polish inflates tester satisfaction, so measure task success. |
| 9 | **Usability Test** | Hawthorne Effect; Observer-Expectancy Effect; Curse of Knowledge; Self-Serving Bias; Survey Bias; Pareto Principle | Tell participants you are testing the product, not them. Stay silent and neutral. Recruit people who have never used the product. Reframe participant self-blame as a design issue. Trust behaviour over post-test ratings. Fix the top 20% of issues that cause most failures. |
| 10 | **Card Sorting** | Mental Model; Curse of Knowledge; Law of Similarity; Chunking; Miller's Law; False Consensus Effect | Let users' groupings replace the company's org-chart taxonomy. Debrief participants on their reasons for each grouping. Keep the top-level category count small and labels in the users' words. |
| 11 | **Survey** | Survey Bias; Framing; Anchoring Bias; Serial Position Effect; Decision Fatigue; Default Bias | Use neutral wording and balanced scales. Randomise option order. Don't let early questions anchor later ones. Keep it short. Never preselect answers. Use it to find where the problem is, then switch to qualitative methods. |
| 12 | **Heuristic Evaluation** | Feedback Loop; Recognition Over Recall; Mental Model; Signifiers; Confirmation Bias; Zeigarnik Effect | Map Nielsen's heuristics onto these principles: system status maps to Feedback Loop, real-world match to Mental Model, recognition to Recognition Over Recall, affordances to Signifiers. Use several independent evaluators to counter expert bias. Judge whether reading-goal prompts help or nag. |
| 13 | **Diary Study** | Internal Trigger; External Trigger; Variable Reward; Investment Loops; Hawthorne Effect; Availability Heuristic | Code entries into a Hook model (trigger, action, reward, investment) to explain habits. Discount the novelty spike from self-logging. Prompt entries close to the moment to avoid memory distortion. |
| 14 | **Design System** | Law of Similarity; Familiarity Bias; Cognitive Load; Aesthetic-Usability Effect; Signifiers; Law of the Instrument | Same function should get the same look. Use constrained tokens. Give interactive states consistent signifiers. Allow new patterns when existing components don't fit, instead of forcing reuse. |
| 15 | **Accessibility** | Empathy Gap; Contrast; Fitts's Law; Signifiers; Cognitive Load; Curse of Knowledge | Test with assistive tech, not only automated scans. Meet WCAG contrast. Use 44px+ targets. Never rely on colour alone. Simplify dense booking steps and avoid harsh timers. Label icons. |
| 16 | **Form** | Signifiers; Feedback Loop; Law of Proximity; Cognitive Load; Default Bias; Fitts's Law | Make each control's look match its behaviour (toggle = instant, checkbox = submit). Validate inline. Keep labels next to fields. Use few fields and chunked groups. Choose defaults ethically. Use large tap areas. |
| 17 | **Information Architecture** | Mental Model; Hick's Law; Chunking; Miller's Law; Progressive Disclosure; Recognition Over Recall; Serial Position Effect | Build task-based nav that matches renters' thinking. Keep top-level choices few. Group pages into clear categories. Reveal depth progressively. Keep nav labels visible. Put the most important items first or last. |
| 18 | **Onboarding** | Aha! Moment; Progressive Disclosure; Goal Gradient Effect; Reciprocity; Loss Aversion; Authority Bias; Social Proof | Get to the first successful payment fast. Introduce features in context. Use a setup checklist with progress. Give value and an explanation before asking for contacts or permissions. Disclose fees early to avoid a feeling of loss later. Show security credentials and "friends already here" to build trust for money. |

**Cross-cutting practice drills:**
- Before any research challenge (1–5, 9–13), write down which 3 biases you are most at risk of and how the method controls each one.
- Before any build challenge (6–8, 14–18), annotate each screen with the principle each key decision relies on, and one principle you deliberately chose *not* to exploit (for example Scarcity or Default Bias) for ethical reasons.
- Measure with the extra credit: onboarding metrics (activation and time-to-value), survey rationale logs, and 5+ participants. Together these show the Pareto Principle and diminishing returns in practice.
