# E — growth.design: case studies, UX in 60 seconds, Product Psychology course, About

## Batch overview
- Scope: 47 listed case studies on growth.design/case-studies (all have a full "text version" of their comic slides), plus 10 legacy/gated case studies still referenced from growth.design/psychology (Airbnb, Superhuman, Tesla, Calm, Facebook, Mario Kart, Spotify vs Apple Ep.1 & Ep.2, Morning Brew, Strava freemium), plus UX in 60 Seconds, the Product Psychology Masterclass (full module/lesson outline recovered from the lesson-sample page) and /about. Total: 61 items.
- House method: every case study is a first-person comic "story" through a real flow, with a running **Psych Level** meter (Darius Contractor's Psych Framework: motivation minus friction), 📌 principle callouts, a redesign, a final letter grade (often an "Ethics" or "Humane Experience" score) and a downloadable cheat sheet/checklist.
- Dominant themes: (1) onboarding: earn trust before asking (permissions, data, signup), get to the Aha! moment early, use Jobs-to-be-Done and personalisation; (2) retention: triggers, streaks, exit points, peak-end endings; (3) revenue: paywalls, upgrades, checkout, trial design done *ethically*; (4) an ethics thread since 2021 with dark-pattern call-outs (shaming, fake scarcity, roach-motel cancellation, privacy washing, rigged rewards).
- Most frequently illustrated principles: Peak-End Rule, Reactance, Framing, Priming, Progressive Disclosure, Hick's Law, Cognitive Load, Endowment Effect, Labor Illusion, Loss Aversion, Reciprocity, Default Bias (status quo), Scarcity, Provide Exit Points, Spark Effect, Singularity Effect.
- Recurring frameworks that are NOT in the 106-principle list and are worth adding as concepts: Psych Framework, B=MAP (Fogg Behavior Model), B.I.A.S. (Block, Interpret, Act, Store), System 1/System 2, Jobs-to-be-Done, 6P Storytelling, Journey mapping (Peaks, Pits, Jumps, Drops, Transitions), Sniper Link, Deferred Account Creation, Persona-based onboarding, Happy Path, Stopping Cues, Habituation, Bystander Effect, Closure, Regret Aversion, Status Quo Bias, Conway's Law, User Shaming, Privacy Washing, Transparency/Intrusiveness (T.I.) ratio, Regret test and Black Mirror test.
- Note: the site gives an "Ethics/Experience score" to most studies. Low scores went to McDonald's kiosks (D), TikTok feed (D, humane), Temu (E), GoDaddy (E), Apple/Meta privacy (E) and Adobe trial (F). Treat those as anti-pattern references.

    - 1.2 Behavior mapping (5h): BMAP, B = M·A·P (Motivation, Ability, Prompt; Fogg).
    - 1.3 Psych framework (3h).

    - 2.1 The B.I.A.S. Framework (1.5h).
    - 2.2 Block (3h), 2.3 Interpret (2h), 2.4 Act (3h), 2.5 Store (3h).
    - 2.6 B.I.A.S. case study recap (2h).
    - B.I.A.S. maps the brain's processing stages (Block, Interpret, Act, Store) and ties them to System 1 (fast) and System 2 (slow) thinking.
  - **Module 3 — Create delightful journeys** (aligned with business goals):
    - 3.1 Mapping journeys (3h): peaks, pits, jumps, drops and transitions.
    - 3.2 Improving journeys (4h): raise the peaks, fix the biggest pit, mark the transitions.
  - **Module 4 — Communicate product decisions**:
    - 4.1 Justify decisions (4h): use psychology to rally stakeholders.
  - **Module 5 — Ethics**:
    - 5.1 Making ethical decisions (3h): the Regret test and the Black Mirror test.
  - **Module 6 — Certification**: final exam (1h) and certificate.
  - Lesson format: Learn (story slides, summary, extra resources), then Practice (an exercise with a Figma template and a solution), then Apply (to your own product).
  - Bonus tools: a product checklist, a one-page cheat sheet, D.I.Y. case-study assets (Figma template) and Psych Variation Benchmarks (positive and negative).
  - Sample lesson 1.3 content:
    - Psych = M·A: the Behavior Map's motivation and ability axes merged into one level.
    - Every interaction adds or removes Psych (Net Perceived Value = Motivation − Friction, after the expected-utility-minus-interaction-cost idea).
    - Picture the user as a video-game hero whose Psych is their health bar.
    - "Good friction" that lines up with motivation can raise conversion, so adding a valuable step can help.
    - Don't over-quantify Psych. It is a mental model, so focus on the insights.
    - Validate it with SEQs (Single Ease Questions).
  - Course positioning: an explicit "not for you if you want dark patterns" stance. Its AI-era pitch is to use psychology to catch what AI-generated designs miss.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Run a Psych analysis on any flow: screenshot each step, write the user's inner monologue, and give each step a ± Psych badge. Then fix the biggest drops.
  - Audit screens with B.I.A.S.: what gets Blocked (ignored), what gets Interpreted (meaning), what Act needs, and what gets Stored (memory).
  - Map journeys for peaks, pits and transitions. Fix the deepest pit, raise one peak, and smooth the transitions.
  - Before shipping, run the Regret test (would users regret this if they understood it?) and the Black Mirror test (what would this look like if it went dark at scale?).
- Psychology links:
  - Cognitive Load — B.I.A.S. "Block" is about what users filter out because of limited mental resources.
  - Mental Model — Module 1 teaches three mental models (Stories, BMAP, Psych) for reasoning about behaviour.
  - Peak-End Rule — Module 3's journey-improvement method rests on peaks and endings.
  - Storytelling Effect — 6P stories and story-based lessons build empathy and recall.
  - Self-Initiated Triggers — BMAP's Prompt component covers triggers, including self-set ones.
  - Noble Edge Effect — the ethics module frames humane design as a long-term advantage.
- New concepts:
  - Psych Framework — a user's momentary motivational "fuel" (Psych = Motivation × Ability). Each step adds or subtracts it, and conversion fails when it runs out.
  - B=MAP / Fogg Behavior Model — a behaviour happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt converge.
  - B.I.A.S. — Block, Interpret, Act, Store: the stages of how the brain handles a UI, used to find gaps.
  - 6P Storytelling — a growth.design structure for customer stories that build empathy.
  - Journey mapping (Peaks/Pits/Jumps/Drops/Transitions) — a way to find emotional highs, lows and hand-offs.
  - System 1/System 2 — Kahneman's fast/instinctive versus slow/deliberate thinking.
  - Regret test / Black Mirror test — ethics checks for product decisions.
  - SEQ (Single Ease Question) — a one-question post-task difficulty rating.

## 5 Deadly Onboarding Mistakes You Should Avoid (Sleepzy)
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/5-product-onboarding-mistakes-to-avoid
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2019-07-03, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - Story: a sleep app (Sleepzy) burns through the user's Psych during onboarding until they delete it.
  - Mistake 1, early notification ask: requesting permission before any value or trust. Ask after the first Aha! moment.
  - Mistake 2, vague copy ("relevant notifications"): spell out exactly what will be sent.
  - Mistake 3, forgetting the "promised land": keep reminding users of the outcome they came for (waking up refreshed).
  - Mistake 4, fake scarcity: limited-time pressure that is not real is a dark pattern.
  - Mistake 5, forced nudges that trigger reactance (e.g. pushing ads or a paywall).
  - Stat: about 21% of users abandon an app after its first use (Localytics).
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Put permission prompts after the first value moment and list concretely what users will receive.
  - Restate the user's goal on every onboarding screen.
  - Never invent time or quantity limits.
- Psychology links:
  - Reciprocity — give value before asking for permissions.
  - Scarcity — shown as an unethical use when the limits are fake.
  - Reactance — users rebel when they feel their choices are taken away.
  - Aha! Moment — the right timing for asks is after the Aha! moment.
  - Expectations Bias — vague copy creates suspicion. Specific copy sets accurate expectations.
- References & resources: NN/g Scarcity Principle — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scarcity-principle-ux/ ; Reactance (Wikipedia) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactance_(psychology) ; Localytics retention — http://info.localytics.com/blog/mobile-apps-whats-a-good-retention-rate

## Adobe: The Psychology of User Offboarding
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/adobe-cancel-subscription
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2021-08-23, Growth.Design (Most Popular)
- Summary:
  - Cancelling Creative Cloud exposes "annual, paid monthly" plans with early-termination fees. The extra revenue comes at the cost of brand trust.
  - Status quo bias is exploited by hiding renewal details and how to cancel ("zombie customers").
  - Repeated discounts and anchors at cancellation train customers to threaten to churn and cheapen the brand (the "sleazy salesman" effect).
  - A forced churn survey that is visibly ignored is worse than asking nothing.
  - Unreasonable friction to leave, compared with Adobe's one-step express checkout to join, is a dark pattern (a roach motel).
  - Recommendations: use Noble Edge (genuine care, e.g. pandemic job losses), give a clean exit point, and end on a positive note.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Make cancellation about as easy as signup. Keep any save offer to a single, honest step.
  - If you ask why someone is leaving, act on it visibly.
  - Design the last screen of offboarding as a good ending (confirmation, data export, "come back anytime").
- Psychology links:
  - Default Bias — status-quo inertia keeps users subscribed when renewal terms are hidden.
  - Anchoring Bias — discount anchors at cancellation buy short-term saves but damage how the brand is perceived.
  - Noble Edge Effect — genuine social responsibility earns loyalty.
  - Peak-End Rule — offboarding is the "end" that shapes long-term memory and word of mouth.
  - Provide Exit Points — let users leave with a sense of completion.
  - Fitts's Law — the psychology library cites this study for button size and placement in the cancellation flow.
- New concepts: Roach Motel (easy in, hard out); Zombie customers (paying but unaware or inactive).
- References & resources: Profitwell, zombie customers — https://www.profitwell.com/recur/all/subscription-renewal ; Discount disadvantages (B. Gaille) — https://brandongaille.com/15-psychological-pricing-advantages-and-disadvantages/ ; HubSpot, act on feedback — https://blog.hubspot.com/service/ask-for-feedback-and-act-on-it/ ; Decision Lab, Noble Edge — https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/noble-edge-effect/ ; TechCrunch, dark patterns — https://techcrunch.com/2018/07/01/wtf-is-dark-pattern-design/ ; User Offboarding Checklist (bonus) — https://bit.ly/3gmfCfJ

## Adobe: The growing issue with "Free" trials UX
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/adobe-trial-ux
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2024-02-05, Growth.Design (Trial score: F)
- Summary:
  - A "free" trial that asks for card details, a long-term commitment and sensitive information before the user sees the product (the "free" trial paradox).
  - An upsell-heavy pricing page right after signup creates high cognitive load at the moment of first value.
  - Pricing complexity (the monthly-but-annual trap) creates an illusion of choice. It is unethical when combined with hard cancellation.
  - Fabricated personalisation (unrelated "deals for you") undermines trust.
  - Counter-example, Rows (value-first): after turning the product itself into the homepage, sign-up conversion rose 72%, and sandbox users activated 30% more.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Let users try the core value before asking for an account or a card.
  - Keep the plan count low and make the real cost and term explicit.
  - Personalise only from real signals.
- Psychology links:
  - Cognitive Load — complex upsell screens drain attention at the critical first moment.
  - Aha! Moment — value-first trials get users to the Aha! moment before any commitment.
  - Reciprocity — give value first, then ask.
  - Decoy Effect — complex tiers can be built to herd users. The study flags this when it hides the true cost.
- New concepts: Fabricated personalisation; Value-first / loginless onboarding (sandbox account).
- References & resources: Lincoln Murphy, SaaS trial benchmarks — https://sixteenventures.com/saas-free-trial-benchmarks ; Fake personalisation — https://clearsalesmessage.com/fake-personalisation/ ; Quolum, cancelling SaaS — https://quolum.com/blog/saas/cancelling-saas-subscriptions-the-good-the-bad-and-the-ugly/ ; LogRocket, free trial UX — https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/how-to-improve-free-trial-experience/ ; Growth Unhinged, PLG benchmarks — https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-plg-benchmarks ; Rows, loginless experience — https://rows.com/blog/post/building-a-loginless-experience-for-1b-people

## The Psychology Behind Amazon's Purchase Experience
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/amazon-purchase-ux
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2022-01-30, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - "Subscribe & Save" is preselected and takes about a third of the screen. The one-time purchase is a tiny radio button, which makes it a dark-pattern default.
  - Fitts's Law is used to steer people: the large target is easy to hit and the small one is hard.
  - A 5–15% discount may be a strong enough reason to break status-quo shopping habits.
  - Second-order effect: if everyone has multiple recurring deliveries a month, the result is overconsumption and shipping waste.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Defaults should match the most likely user goal, not the business's best case. Give alternatives equal visual weight.
  - Think through what your nudge does at population scale (second-order effects).
- Psychology links:
  - Default Bias — the preselected subscription relies on inertia.
  - Fitts's Law — target size and distance decide which option gets picked.
  - Nudge — an example where the nudge tips into a dark pattern.
  - Second-Order Effect — the downstream environmental and consumption impact.
  - Social Proof — the psychology library cites this study (Amazon shows the "most common choice").
- References & resources: Brignull, Dark Patterns — https://www.darkpatterns.org/ ; Decision Lab, status quo bias — https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/status-quo-bias/

## Amber Alert Redesign: 5 UX Improvements That Could Save Lives
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/amber-alert-ux
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2022-07-12, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - Habituation: repeated, identical, loud alerts lose their effect ("the boy who cried wolf").
  - Phone alerts are about 8x less likely to resolve a case than radio, TV or road signs, even though about 97% of Americans own a cellphone. In Canada, alerts can't be opted out of.
  - Redesign: add the child's photo, since recognising a face beats recalling a description.
  - Show one identifiable child to trigger empathy (singularity).
  - Overlay the user's own location on the zone to counter the bystander effect.
  - Send a closure notification when the child is found, so people see their help matters.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Vary and personalise high-stakes notifications so they don't habituate users.
  - Use images, a single identifiable person and personal relevance to drive prosocial action.
  - Close the loop by reporting outcomes.
- Psychology links:
  - Priming — visual cues (a photo) prime faster recognition.
  - Recognition Over Recall — a face is easier to recognise than a text description is to recall.
  - Singularity Effect — one face moves people more than statistics.
  - Spotlight Effect — placing "you" on the map makes it personally relevant.
  - Banner Blindness — repeated alerts get tuned out (habituation).
  - Zeigarnik Effect — the need for closure on unresolved situations motivates attention to the resolution.
- New concepts: Habituation — reacting less to a repeated stimulus; Bystander Effect — feeling less responsible when others could help; Closure — the need for a definite resolution.
- References & resources: Habituation (VeryWellMind) — https://www.verywellmind.com/what-is-habituation-2795233 ; Behavioral habituation (NCBI) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2754195 ; Amber Alert stats (OJP) — https://amberalert.ojp.gov/statistics ; Pew mobile fact sheet — https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/ ; Face perception (NCBI) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2629401/ ; Walking in Sabina's Shoes (World Vision video) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-bEtqZoD4V4 ; Bystander effect — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect ; Closure — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_(psychology) ; Duhigg, how habits work — https://charlesduhigg.com/how-habits-work/ ; Financial Post, fix Amber Alerts — https://financialpost.com/opinion/philip-cross-we-need-to-fix-the-amber-alert-system

## Apple vs Meta Threads: The Illusion of Privacy
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/apple-privacy-policy
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2023-09-04, Growth.Design (Ethics and privacy score: E)
- Summary:
  - Threads couldn't launch in the EU at first because its tracking was too invasive for GDPR.
  - The Threads signup framing highlights benefits and hides that deleting Threads means deleting your Instagram account (a roach motel).
  - The T.I. ratio: transparency should scale with how intrusive the data request is.
  - Apple's "Ask App Not To Track" is privacy washing: fingerprinting continues around the IDFA.
  - Incentives drive behaviour, and privacy is an unpriced externality. A prominent privacy score on app pages would change what people download.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - The more sensitive the data you ask for, the more plainly you must explain it, up front.
  - Don't label a control "privacy" unless it actually stops the tracking.
- Psychology links:
  - Framing — how a benefit is presented can hide its real cost.
  - Default Bias — data sharing that is on by default.
  - Affect Heuristic — a reassuring privacy UI creates a feeling of safety that substitutes for actual analysis.
  - Nudge — a front-and-centre privacy score is a positive nudge.
- New concepts: Privacy washing — appearing to protect privacy without doing so; T.I. ratio — Transparency must be proportional to Intrusiveness; Externality — a cost borne by others that isn't priced in.
- References & resources: CJEU ruling on Meta (DLA Piper) — https://www.dlapiper.com/en-AT/insights/publications/2023/07/cjeus-landmark-decision-in-meta-vs-bundeskartellamt ; Control & transparency (UX Mag) — https://uxmag.com/articles/control-and-transparency ; Roach motel — https://blog.mobiversal.com/dark-patterns-or-how-ux-exploits-the-user-roach-motel-and-sneak-into-basket.html ; Mozilla privacy washing — https://foundation.mozilla.org/en/blog/privacy-nightmare-on-wheels-every-car-brand-reviewed-by-mozilla-including-ford-volkswagen-and-toyota-flunks-privacy-test/ ; Oxford, iOS ATT study — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359812579_Goodbye_Tracking_Impact_of_iOS_App_Tracking_Transparency_and_Privacy_Labels ; WaPo iPhone tracking — https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2021/09/23/iphone-tracking/ ; Recode hidden trackers — https://www.vox.com/recode/2020/7/8/21311533/sdks-tracking-data-location ; fs.blog incentives — https://fs.blog/bias-incentives-reinforcement/ ; Externality — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality

## One simple way Apple could improve your sleep habits
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/apple-sleep-notification
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2024-04-30, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - The iOS wind-down notification looks like every other notification, so it gets tuned out (banner blindness).
  - "Positive friction" is good when it protects the user's own goal (e.g. a deliberate swipe to start wind-down).
  - Spark effect: a tiny action (one swipe) commits the user to the routine and shows progress toward optimal sleep.
  - Loss aversion: frame the downside as losing sleep preparation, but only when the loss is real.
  - Cites Tim Cook: the phone should improve life, not maximise use.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Give important recurring prompts a distinct, novel format.
  - Add deliberate friction where it serves the user's long-term goal.
  - Start routines with a micro-commitment.
- Psychology links:
  - Banner Blindness — identical notification formats get ignored.
  - Spark Effect — a small first step starts the behaviour.
  - Loss Aversion — losing earned progress motivates more than gaining it.
  - Reactance — a fake or pushy loss frame is rejected.
  - Commitment & Consistency — the swipe acts as a commitment that the rest of the routine follows.
- New concepts: Positive friction — friction that protects the user's goal.
- References & resources: Novelty (Brain World) — https://brainworldmagazine.com/the-importance-of-novelty/ ; Tim Cook interview (CBS) — https://youtu.be/AwvLymAA2CM?t=237

## Audible: Build seamless purchase experiences
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/audible-purchase-ux
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2025-01-20, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - After buying credits, users don't see them right away ("where are my credits?") and must wait. Reassurance is missing.
  - Lack of post-purchase reassurance creates unease and erodes trust at the start of the relationship.
  - The moments right after payment are a neglected gap (Samuel Hulick's "mind the gap") and a chance for the brand to shine.

  - Design the transition into first use (e.g. "start listening" right away).

  - Feedback Loop — users need immediate confirmation that an action worked.
  - Cognitive Load — fewer steps and less to work out after purchase.
  - Peak-End Rule — purchase confirmation is a key end or transition moment.
  - Cognitive Dissonance — post-purchase doubt (buyer's remorse) has to be soothed.
- New concepts: Post-purchase reassurance; Successful transitions (bridging the post-conversion gap).

## The Psychology of Advertising: Why this ad made me stop scrolling (Balance app)
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/balance-psychology
- Type: case-study
- Date/author: 2022-05-01, Growth.Design
- Summary:
  - An Instagram ad for the Balance meditation app looks like an iOS Notes page. The familiar layout puts the brain into "reading my notes" mode.
  - Because it looks different from surrounding photo ads, it stands out (Von Restorff).
  - Its copy about wasting time on your phone matched what the viewer was already thinking about (attentional bias).
  - Ogilvy: be informative, not clever. Clever copy gets misread.
  - Customer fatigue: overused tactics stop working, the way Kano delighters decay into expectations.
- Actionable takeaways for a designer:
  - Use familiar, native-looking formats to get past ad filters, while staying honest about what you are.
  - Design for contrast with the surrounding context, not in isolation.
  - Speak to whatever is already on the audience's mind.
- Psychology links:
  - Familiarity Bias — a familiar layout lowers resistance and raises attention.

## How Blinkist Increased Trial Conversions by 23% (Ethically)
- URL: https://growth.design/case-studies/trial-paywall-challenge
- Type: case-study (challenge)
- Date/author: 2021-12-15, Growth.Design

  - Reactance — forced behaviour causes resistance.
  - Goal Gradient Effect — endowed progress speeds completion.
  - Loss Aversion — fear of being charged is the key barrier.
  - Framing — a transparent timeline reframes the trial as safe.
  - Expectations Bias — fine print triggers learned suspicion.
